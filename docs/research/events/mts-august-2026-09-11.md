## Initial research

### The question, plainly

What is the Monthly Treasury Statement, is the 2026-09-11 date real, what will the August 2026 edition
say, does it move anything, and how should a paper-trading book that holds long-duration AI names
(NVDA MRVL AVGO CRWV MSFT GOOG META AAPL AMZN) use a monthly deficit print at all?

**One-line verdict:** the date is now primary-`confirmed` at 2:00 p.m. ET and the print is a measured
market non-event — its headline is pre-computable from daily Treasury data (this session computed it:
**~$230B**, band $200–265B, against $344.8B a year ago) and essentially all of the y/y improvement is
payment-calendar arithmetic plus a decaying court-ordered tariff-refund wave, so the only honest use
of this document is as a monthly fiscal *input* to the long-end ledgers, never as an event to trade.

### Method

Macro-print mode per [`EVENT-RESEARCH.md`](../../process/EVENT-RESEARCH.md) — `symbols: []`, so the
symbol-keyed instruments (`earnings-cycle.mjs`, `intraday-edges.mjs`) do not apply and the
cache-busting rule has no target. Everything below is primary except where flagged:

- **Treasury Fiscal Data API**, fetched direct 2026-09-03 (plain `curl`, HTTP 200 on every call):
  `accounting/mts/mts_table_1` for the FY2025/FY2026 monthly deficit series;
  `accounting/dts/deposits_withdrawals_operating_cash` for twelve month-end Daily Treasury Statement
  snapshots; `od/debt_to_penny`; `od/avg_interest_rates`.
- **The MTS publications themselves**, PDFs downloaded from
  `fiscaldata.treasury.gov/static-data/published-reports/mts/` and text-extracted with `pdftotext
  -layout`: the **July 2026** edition (its Highlight box and its Scheduled Release page), the **June
  2026** edition, the **November 2025** edition (which carries the full 2026 release calendar) and the
  **November 2023** edition (the 2024 calendar).
- **Market data**: Yahoo daily and hourly bars for TLT, SPY and ^TNX, 200,000-iteration two-sided
  permutation tests, hourly bars bucketed to `America/New_York`.
- **Secondary, and labeled as such**: the Supreme Court IEEPA ruling and the CIT refund order are
  press- and law-firm-reported (cbo.gov returns a JS-challenge wall to `curl` and 403 to WebFetch, so
  CBO's own Monthly Budget Review could not be read this session).

### Conviction legs, tested

1. **The date is real, the time is 2:00 p.m. ET, and the entry is PROMOTED to `confirmed` —
   SUPPORTED by two independent Treasury primaries.** The July 2026 MTS, page 39, reads verbatim:
   *"The release date for the August 2026 Statement will be 2:00 p.m. EST September 11, 2026."*
   ("EST" is Treasury's own typo; September is EDT.) Independently, the November 2025 edition carries
   the full annual table — *"Listed below are the scheduled release dates for the Monthly Treasury
   Statement. The Statement is released at 2:00 p.m."* — with **August 2026 → September 11, 2026**.
   `TSY:` is an authorized confirmed-tier prefix and these are Treasury's own publications, so the
   `estimate` label filed on 2026-09-03 no longer has grounds. **Two corrections to the entry as
   filed**, both banked honestly: it claimed the July edition published **2026-08-13** and that this
   was *"itself the 8th August workday."* Treasury's own schedule says **August 12** in two separate
   PDFs, and August 13 is the **9th** August workday, not the 8th — the arithmetic was wrong in a way
   that happened not to change the answer.

2. **The 8th-workday rule is exact, not approximate — SUPPORTED, and it is now a reusable tool.**
   Computing the 8th federal workday of each month against the published schedules reproduces **20 of
   20** dates across the 2024 and 2026 calendars with **zero** mismatches. That is what let this doc
   date all 56 historical releases for leg 4 without fetching 56 PDFs, and it is why the "subject to
   change" caveat on Treasury's table is not a real risk here.

3. **The August deficit is already knowable, and this session computed it — SUPPORTED.** The MTS's own
   Introduction calls the Daily Treasury Statement part of the same "triad," published every working
   day. Summing DTS operating-cash flows for August 2026 and stripping public-debt issuance and
   redemptions gives **deposits $420.9B, withdrawals $623.0B, net gap $202.1B**. That is not the MTS
   number — the MTS is modified-cash with accrued interest on public issues — so the gap was
   calibrated against eleven months where both exist:

   | ^TNX | 56 | +0.054% | 30/56 | **0.839** | +0.108% |

   volatility premium, nothing. The mechanism in leg 3 explains it: there is no surprise to react to.

5. **August 2026 is a zero-benefit-batch month and August 2025 held two — SUPPORTED by Treasury's own

6. **The tariff-refund wave is the one genuinely new fact, and it is decaying fast — SUPPORTED on

   SUPPORTED.** Ten-month totals: FY2026 receipts **$4,485.4B**, outlays **$6,284.2B**, deficit

### What the conditions support

Nothing directional. What it supports is a **reading discipline and a calendar habit**:

- **Skip the headline; read two lines.** The CBP refund outflow (is the one-off finished?) and net
  interest (is the structural cost still compounding?). Both feed the long-end ledgers directly.
- **Discount the y/y comparison to zero for August.** Two mechanical effects — zero benefit batches
  against a two-batch prior year, and a refund wave decaying from $51B to $12B — will make August look
  like fiscal consolidation. It is not.
- **Use the DTS instead of waiting.** Leg 3 shows the number is derivable ten days early to ±$32B from
  a free public API. For any future month where the deficit matters to a decision, compute it; do not
  wait on the 8th workday.
- **Do not price event risk into 9/11 afternoon.** Leg 4 measured the release window as quieter than
  an ordinary afternoon.

### Honest limits

The $230B estimate is a **calibrated mapping, not a forecast of Treasury's arithmetic** — its σ is
$31.9B on eleven observations, and one of those eleven (Feb 2026, −$33.6B) sits nearly 2σ the wrong
way, so a print at $190B or $275B would be unusual but not disqualifying. The single prior *August*
in the calibration set maps at **−$4.6B**, which alone would imply ~$198B; the doc quotes the pooled
figure and flags the disagreement rather than picking the flattering one. The month-end DTS snapshots
use each month's last business day, which for a month ending on a weekend is not the calendar month
end — a known small source of mapping error, not corrected here. The IEEPA refund story's **cause**
(SCOTUS 6–3 on 2026-02-20, ~$165B CIT order, CAPE phase one 2026-04-20) is entirely
press/law-firm-sourced: cbo.gov serves a JavaScript challenge to `curl` and 403 to WebFetch, so CBO's
own Monthly Budget Review — which would both corroborate the refund figures and publish days *before*
this print — could not be read; it is a known leading release this calendar still cannot date, and it
is deliberately **not** proposed as a calendar entry on an unverified date. The CBP withdrawal line
also carries ordinary agency disbursements, so $136.5B FYTD is an upper bound on refunds, not a
refund total. The August interest y/y comparison is calendar-contaminated in the opposite direction to
the benefits (2025-08-31 fell on a Sunday) and is quoted as context only. The market-impact null is
measured on TLT/SPY/^TNX close data and a 1:30 p.m. window that includes 30 minutes of pre-release
drift; no options-implied or MOVE-based test was run, and no attempt was made to separate MTS days
that collided with CPI from those that did not — with n=35 that split would not have been powered.
