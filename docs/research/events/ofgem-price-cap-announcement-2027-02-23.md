## At a glance

**TL;DR.** This is the first Ofgem cap that must price domestic electricity back at **5% VAT**, and that
is the only reason it is on this calendar. **The proposal that created it sized the effect as "~5%";
the honest number is about half that on the figure anyone will actually read.** Ofgem published its own
counterfactual on 26 August 2026 — *"Without the Government's intervention on VAT, this figure would have
been around **£45** higher"* — and £45 on the £1,723 typical dual-fuel cap is **+2.6% of the headline**.
Cornwall Insight corroborates from the other side (*"save around **£44**"*, 21 July 2026). The 5% is real
but it lands on the electricity leg only, roughly half the dual-fuel bill. **The second correction is
bigger: 2027-02-23 is not where this is decided.** Whether the snap-back happens at all turns on whether
SI 2026/987 gets extended, and the Government scoped that to *"the Budget alongside an OBR forecast"* —
**2026-10-28**, one hundred and eighteen days earlier. By the time Ofgem announces, the tax answer is four
months old and Ofgem is pricing a decision, not making one. And as with its sibling, the number itself is
arithmetic **~7 days before** the announcement: the observation window closes **2027-02-16** — a
construction validated by reproducing Cornwall Insight's dated 18 November 2025 window close exactly.
**None of this reaches this book.** `symbols: []`; the calendar's universe is eight US mega-caps with no
UK listing, utility, gilt or sterling leg, and no house playbook is energy- or sterling-keyed. The date is

### Leg 1 — "a mechanical ~5% snap-back" — **MIXED**, and the correction is the headline finding

The proposal wrote that this announcement "carries a wholesale move AND a mechanical ~5% snap-back on the
electricity leg in one number." The **leg** claim is correct. The trouble is that the "one number" Ofgem
publishes and every outlet reports is the **dual-fuel** typical annual bill, and 5% of the electricity leg
is not 5% of that.

Ofgem removed the guesswork by publishing the counterfactual itself. From the 26 August 2026 press release
(fetched direct):

> "Without the Government's intervention on VAT, this figure would have been around **£45** higher."

The figure in question is the **£1,723** typical dual-fuel direct-debit annual cap. So:

| Quantity | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Oct–Dec 2026 cap (typical dual fuel, DD) | **£1,723** (+4%, +£60) | Ofgem press release, 26 Aug 2026 |
| VAT zero-rating's contribution | **~£45** | Ofgem, same release, verbatim |
| Implied headline effect | **+2.61%** | £45 / £1,723 |
| Implied VAT-exclusive electricity leg | **~£900** (≈52% of the bill) | £45 / 0.05 |

Cornwall Insight reached the same magnitude from the opposite direction and five weeks earlier, on
21 July 2026: *"Households are set to save around **£44** on a typical dual-fuel energy bill."* Two
independent parties, **£44–45**.

**So the number to carry forward is ~£45, or ~+2.6% of headline — not ~5%.** The distinction is not
pedantry: a reader who applies 5% to the headline overstates the mechanical component by roughly a factor
of two, and will then under-attribute the rest of the move to wholesale. That is exactly the attribution
error this entry exists to prevent.

**Honest limit.** Both figures are quoted on the Oct–Dec 2026 consumption basis and at that quarter's
consumption mix. The April–June quarter is a *summer* quarter — lower absolute consumption, and a
different electricity/gas ratio than winter. The £45 is therefore the right order of magnitude and the
right method, not a forecast of the exact April pound figure. `FT-…-1` is written as a **£35–£55 band**
for precisely that reason.

### Leg 2 — "the April step needs a control quarter to decompose" — **REFUTED**

The sibling ledger, proposing the Jul–Sep 2027 cap, wrote that "without the second one the April step
cannot be decomposed into tax versus wholesale." Testing that against how Ofgem actually writes these
releases refutes it.

Ofgem's Apr–Jun 2026 cap release (fetched direct) attributes the change to named cost components with
pound figures:

> customers "will save an average of **£150**" from the change in how environmental and social schemes are
> funded; "global wholesale energy prices have also gone down by **£38** a year"; "Network costs have
> increased by **£66** a year".

And the Oct–Dec 2026 release named the VAT effect in pounds **unprompted**, as quoted in Leg 1. A regulator
that already itemises policy, wholesale and network — and that has already shown it will state the VAT
counterfactual — will not leave the single largest and most politically salient component of the April 2027
move unattributed.

**The Jul–Sep 2027 cap is still worth tracking**, as an out-of-sample check on whatever decomposition Ofgem
publishes and as the first fully post-reversion quarter. But it is not load-bearing for reading the April
number, and the sibling's stated reason for it does not survive. Registered as `FT-…-2` rather than argued.

### Leg 3 — "the snap-back is Ofgem's to make" — **REFUTED**, and this is the most decision-relevant leg

Ofgem sets a cap; it does not set VAT. Whether the Apr–Jun 2027 cap carries 5% electricity VAT depends
entirely on whether SI 2026/987 lapses on schedule — and the
[`uk-electricity-vat-reversion-2027-04-01`](uk-electricity-vat-reversion-2027-04-01.md) lane already
established the two facts that matter, from primaries it fetched:

1. The Order "has effect only for supplies made in the period beginning with 1st October 2026 and ending
   with 31st March 2027" — **no further act is needed** for 5% to resume.
2. An **extension** is procedurally cheap: it creates or continues a zero-rated group and so escapes
   VATA 1994 s.97(4)(c)(iii)'s affirmative-vote requirement — a **negative-procedure SI, layable at any
   time up to 2027-03-31, no vote required**.

And the Government scoped the decision itself, in its own 21 July 2026 words: *"Any further action,
including on funding for longer-term measures, will be taken at the **Budget** alongside an OBR forecast."*

The Budget is [`uk-autumn-budget-2026-10-28`](uk-autumn-budget-2026-10-28.md) — **118 days before this
announcement**. So the ordering is:

| Date | What happens | Days to the cap |
|---|---|---|
| **2026-10-28** | Autumn Budget — where the Government said the extension question gets answered | −118 |
| ~**2027-02-16** | Observation window closes; the cap becomes arithmetic | −7 |
| **2027-02-17** | January 2027 UK CPI prints (est) | −6 |
| **2027-02-23** | Ofgem announces the Apr–Jun 2027 cap (ceiling) | 0 |
| **2027-03-31** | Last day an extension SI could be laid | +36 |
| **2027-04-01** | VAT reverts; cap takes effect | +37 |

**By 2027-02-23 the tax answer should be four months old.** The announcement is a *readout* of a policy
decision, not the decision — which extends the sibling's "confirmation, not a print" finding from the
wholesale axis onto the policy axis.

**The residual-risk branch, stated honestly.** The Budget is free to say nothing. If it does, the extension
window stays open for **36 days after** Ofgem announces, and Ofgem would be pricing 5% into a rate that
could still change. The Government's one precedent argues against it — it announced the cut on
**21 July 2026** for a **1 October 2026** start, a 72-day lead, and Ofgem set the October cap on
**26 August 2026** with the policy already in hand — but n=1. **No precedent for Ofgem revising an
already-announced cap was located**, which is a gap in this research, not a finding that none exists.
`FT-…-4` scores the Budget branch by 2026-11-04.

### Leg 4 — "the observation-window date is derived, not verified" (the sibling's softest link) — **now VALIDATED**

**validated against a dated statement** instead, which is nearly as good and was available all along.

Under Ofgem's **3-1.5-12[3]** indexation the observation window closes ~**30 working days** before the cap
period starts. Counting back 30 working days, excluding weekends and UK bank holidays:

| Cap period starts | 30 working days back | Independent check |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-01-01 | **2025-11-18** | Cornwall Insight: *"following the closure of the observation window on **18 November**"* — **exact match** |
| 2027-01-01 | **2026-11-18** | inside the sibling's derived "~17–18 November 2026" |
| **2027-04-01** | **2027-02-16** | this cycle — Good Friday 2027-03-26 and Easter Monday 2027-03-29 fall inside the count |

Reproducing a dated third-party statement exactly, on the one cycle where such a statement exists, is
meaningful evidence that the construction is the right one. **The Apr–Jun 2027 cap is arithmetic on or
about 2027-02-16 — seven days before Ofgem says it**, and one day before the January 2027 UK CPI prints.

The limit that remains is unchanged in kind and smaller in degree: no Ofgem primary naming *this* cycle's
window was fetched, and the bank-holiday treatment inside the count is an assumption about Ofgem's own
working-day convention, not a documented one.

### Leg 5 — "the April cap falls seasonally, so the snap-back will mask a wholesale decline" — **REFUTED**

This was this lane's own hypothesis, and the tape kills it. Two April cycles are sourceable and they point
opposite ways:

| Cap period | Change | Driver Ofgem named |
|---|---|---|
| Apr–Jun 2025 | **+6.4%** to £1,849 | wholesale, ~78% of the increase |
| Apr–Jun 2026 | **−7%** (−£117) to £1,641 | **policy costs −£150**, wholesale −£38, network +£66 |

There is no April seasonal. The 2026 fall was a **policy** event — environmental and social scheme funding
moving off bills — not a seasonal one. Recorded because a plausible-sounding seasonal, left unstated, is
the kind of thing a future pulse re-invents.

What *is* true and worth keeping is the weaker version: the April 2027 headline will contain a **+£45-ish
mechanical uplift** that has nothing to do with wholesale, so a flat headline would mean an underlying
decline. That is arithmetic, not seasonality, and Leg 2 says Ofgem will publish enough to see it.

### A second aggregator claim rejected on the record

The sibling rejected an aggregator's invented observation window. A different one turned up here: a
syndicated headline reports the October 2026 cap as **"£1,935"**. Ofgem's own figure is **£1,723**.

The gap is entirely the **TDCV revision**. Ofgem stated it "reduced the TDCV in keeping with the downward
trend in consumption" in July 2026, citing roughly 7% less electricity and 17% less gas than the 2023
review. Reconstructing from the published Oct–Dec 2026 unit rates — electricity 26.32p/kWh + 54.83p/day
standing, gas 7.97p/kWh + 29.68p/day standing:

- On the **old** basis (2,700 kWh electricity / 11,500 kWh gas): **£1,936** — which is the aggregator's number.
- On the **reduced** basis implied by Ofgem's own percentages (≈2,511 / ≈9,545): **£1,730** — within **£7**
  of Ofgem's published £1,723.

The reconstruction is arithmetic on top of Ofgem's stated percentages, not a fetched TDCV pair, so it is
offered as an **explanation of the discrepancy** rather than as a sourced consumption basis. It is
sufficient to establish which number is stale. **Every cross-cycle comparison in this file is on the
post-July-2026 basis only** — the sibling's honest-limits note about mixed consumption definitions was
right, and this is its resolution.
