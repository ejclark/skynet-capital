## Initial research

### The question

Two sibling sweeps proposed this event on the same day for different reasons.
[`tic-monthly-2027-03-18`](tic-monthly-2027-03-18.md) filed it as a **taxonomy correction** — the
`31` sits in its own dated column and is a separate release, not a third cut of the 03-18 monthly.
[`tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-06-30`](tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-06-30.md) filed it as
**the last checkpoint** before its own forecast scores. Both are about what this event is *for*
somewhere else. Neither asked the question this session owns: **is a March edition anything in
particular, or is it an interchangeable member of a four-times-a-year series?**

### One-line verdict

It is something in particular, and it is the **inverse of the intuitive reading**: the March edition
publishes the number this series revises **least**, on the **only** release date in the series that
has never slipped — and the revision concentration it avoids sits on the end-June quarter, the
survey's own benchmark date, for reasons that are **not** the survey.

### Method

Sourced web research per [`EVENT-RESEARCH.md`](../../process/EVENT-RESEARCH.md) (macro-print mode —
no symbol instruments exist for this kind; `symbols: []`). Primaries fetched **direct** this session
(2026-09-09, plain curl, HTTP 200): Treasury's TIC release-dates table (**111,181 bytes**) including
its full REVISIONS-and-RELEASE-DATES note block; Treasury's *U.S. Gross External Debt* statistics
page (**174,128 bytes**), which carries a per-as-of-quarter index of every current and archived table
vintage; **all 134** archived Table A vintages that index names, fetched from `ticdata.treasury.gov`
(as-of 2009Q1 through 2026Q1, zero fetch failures); Treasury's daily par yield curve CSVs for
**2011–2026** (3,922 sessions); and CBOE's `VIX_History.csv`. Every statistic below was **recomputed
from those files**, independently of the sibling ledger's run.

Each vintage's publication date is read from **inside the file**, never inferred from the index page.
Three stamp formats occur and all three are parsed: the BPM6 footnote
`1/ Source: Department of the Treasury[, revision of] <date>` (90 files), the BPM5 footer
`Revised <date>` (12 files), and a **bare trailing date** with no label (32 files). The bare form is
an inference and is flagged as such in the limits — it is internally coherent (every `debta*` bare
date lands on a scheduled release day, every `deb2a*` bare date lands in June) but it is not a label.
Last-business-day arithmetic was recomputed from `datetime` with 5 U.S.C. 6103 holidays constructed
from the statute's rules rather than a list. `probe-ref.blocked` is empty: no cited source failed.

**A genuine first vintage** is defined here as the earliest surviving vintage for an as-of quarter
whose stamp falls within 45 days of that quarter's scheduled release. The archive retains at most
three vintages per as-of quarter, so **10 of 69** as-of quarters have no surviving first vintage and
are excluded from the offset, durability and level legs rather than guessed at.

### Leg 1 — the date · **SUPPORTED**, and promoted `estimate` → `confirmed`

Treasury's 2027 table puts March's row at **31** in the column headed *Quarterly: U.S. gross external
debt at end of previous quarter* — a separate dated column from the `18` cells in the Monthly and
Quarterly-derivatives columns, which is exactly the read
[`tic-monthly-2027-03-18`](tic-monthly-2027-03-18.md) corrected. The page header reads *"All data
releases occur at 4 p.m. Washington, D.C. time"*, and its note block states the rule: *"The release
dates are the last business day of each quarter (March, June, September, and December)."*
**2027-03-31 is a Wednesday and the last business day of March 2027.**

A second Treasury primary, fetched the same session, is more specific than the release-dates page and
puts **this** release in the 4 PM group by name:

> *"Releases are made at 4 PM in Washington D.C. on the last business day of March, June, and
> September, and at 10 AM on the last business day of December."*

The empirical corroboration is where this event separates from its siblings. Splitting the 59 genuine
first-vintage publications by **which edition** they are:

| Edition | n | at offset +0 | exceptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **March** | **15** | **15** | **none** |
| September | 14 | 14 | none |
| June | 15 | 13 | 2012-06-30 (+1), 2025-07-02 (+2) |
| December | 15 | 12 | 2019-01-31 (+31), 2024-12-30 (−1), 2026-01-16 (+16) |
| **All** | **59** | **54** | 56 within ±1 day |

**Every slip this series has ever had is a June or December release.** The three December exceptions
are the series' worst — the 35-day funding lapse of 2018-12-22 → 2019-01-25 pushed the as-of 2018Q3
release to **2019-01-31**, and the as-of 2025Q3 release landed **2026-01-16**, flagged *"16
(delayed)"* on Treasury's own page. December is also the one edition the statistics page singles out
as **10 AM** rather than 4 PM; whatever operational difference that time reflects, the slip record
tracks it, and **March is on the clean side of it**.

Promotion follows [`tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-06-30`](tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-06-30.md)
and [`tic-annual-survey-prelim-2027-02-26`](tic-annual-survey-prelim-2027-02-26.md): one named
Treasury primary plus independent mechanical corroboration. The `estimate` ground both proposals
cited was this lane's **no-self-confirm** limit — the entry was discovered by two sweeps, not
researched. That is cleared here by this event's own session re-fetching both pages. As on every
sibling, `confirmed` describes **the published schedule and nothing else**: the release-dates page
carries the standing notice that a date *"will be revised after offices reopen"* if Washington D.C.
offices are closed, and the 2019 exception above is that notice firing.

### Leg 2 — does this edition revise anything? · **REFUTED (it does not)**, and the census reproduces

The sibling found **66 of 66** archived revisions carrying a June stamp. That is the kind of claim
worth re-running rather than citing, because it is the load-bearing fact for every non-June edition
in the calendar — including this one.

Re-fetched and re-stamped independently this session: **69** as-of quarters, **59** genuine first
vintages, **75** surviving later vintages. Grouping every later vintage by the **month of its own
publication stamp**:

```
revision vintages by release month, all 134 archived Table A vintages, 2010-2026
  June        75
  March        0
  September    0
  December     0
```

**Seventy-five for seventy-five.** The count differs from the sibling's 66 because the classification
differs — this session counts *every* surviving non-first vintage rather than every step between
consecutive vintages, and dates 134 of 134 files where the sibling dated 65 (the bare-trailing-date
format above is the difference). **The month attribution does not differ at all.** Two independent
runs over the same archive, with different parsers and different definitions of the unit, both find
zero non-June revisions in fifteen years.

The operative consequence for this event is one sentence: **the 2027-03-31 release publishes one new
quarter and restates nothing.** Treasury's own note block says the same thing prospectively —
*"Revised data for the previous five quarters (back to the first quarter of the previous year) are
normally released on the last business day of June"* — and the archive says it has never been
otherwise.

### Leg 3 — what the March edition's number is worth · **SUPPORTED**, and this is the new finding

If June is the only revision edition, then every first vintage waits a different length of time for
its restatement, purely as a function of which quarter it is:

| First vintage | published by | waits | for its June restatement |
|---|---|---|---|
| as-of Q1 | the June edition | **365 days** | the *following* June |
| as-of Q2 | the September edition | 273 days | |
| as-of Q3 | the December edition | 182 days | |
| **as-of Q4** | **the March edition** | **91 days** | the *very next* June |

The intuitive reading is that the March edition's number is the most provisional in the series — it
is barely three months old when the benchmark hits it. **That reading is wrong.** Across the **58**
as-of quarters with both a surviving first vintage and a later one, revision magnitude by as-of
quarter:

| As-of quarter (edition that publishes it) | n | mean \|revision\| | as % of level | median % | mean **signed** % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Q4 — the March edition** | **15** | **$145.0bn** | **0.784%** | **0.600%** | +0.264% |
| Q1 — the June edition | 14 | $157.1bn | 0.792% | 0.921% | +0.444% |
| Q3 — the December edition | 15 | $266.6bn | 1.265% | 0.586% | +0.803% |
| **Q2 — the September edition** | **14** | **$376.4bn** | **1.901%** | **2.501%** | **−1.088%** |

**Q4 vs the other three: t=−1.75, p=0.080** (p=0.151 excluding the 2024Q3 data event, which sits in
Q3 and inflates the comparison group). The as-of-Q4 number is the joint-most durable in the series
while having the least time to be corrected. Stated as the honest strength it has: **directionally the
most durable, at 8% significance on n=15 — a ranking, not a proven law**, which is why FT-…-4
registers it as a *rank* prediction rather than a threshold.

There is also a plain reading of the mean signed column worth keeping: as-of-Q4 is revised **up** on
average by 0.264%, and only as-of-Q2 is revised down. Whatever the June benchmark does, it does not
systematically mark the year-end position lower.

### Leg 4 — where the revisions actually concentrate, and whether the survey explains it · **MIXED → REFUTED**

The table above has an outlier that is not this event's quarter, and it is too well-placed to leave
alone. **As-of-Q2 — the end-June position — is revised at 1.901% against 0.99% pooled for the other
three: t=+2.15, p=0.031** (**p=0.008** excluding the 2024Q3 data event). It is also the **only**
quarter with a negative mean signed revision. And **end-June is precisely the benchmark date of the
Survey of Foreign Holdings of U.S. Securities**, the input Treasury names in its own revision note.

That is a survey fingerprint if anything is. The sibling ledger refuted the survey as the driver of
the June edition's *size*; this is a different axis — *which quarter* the restatement lands on — and
the coincidence of dates is exact. So it gets tested with the same discriminator the sibling built:
Table A's market-valued **Debt Securities: By Sector** memorandum, the one component a securities
survey can move. Across the **45** BPM6 vintage pairs where the memo is readable at both vintages:

| As-of quarter | n | mean \|Δ debt securities\| | mean signed | Σ\|Δsecurities\| ÷ Σ\|Δheadline\| |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q4 | 12 | $358.0bn | +$289.7bn | 2.98 |
| Q3 | 12 | $368.2bn | +$293.4bn | 1.38 |
| Q1 | 11 | $42.0bn | +$3.4bn | 0.26 |
| **Q2 — the survey's own benchmark quarter** | **10** | **$35.4bn** | −$13.8bn | **0.08** |

**Q2 vs the other three: t=−1.51, p=0.132.** The quarter with the largest headline revision has the
**smallest** securities revision of the four, by an order of magnitude, and the securities memo
accounts for **8%** of its headline revision against 298% of Q4's. That is the **wrong sign** for a
survey story. The end-June concentration is real and it lives in loans, deposits, trade credit and
intercompany lending — the banking and direct-investment forms — exactly where the sibling located
the June edition's bulk.

So the survey story fails twice, on two unrelated tests, run by two sessions: it does not explain the
June edition's **size** and it does not explain the June edition's **placement**. What remains is a
June revision convention whose timing is documented and whose composition is banking-side, with the
survey folded in as one input among several.

**Two limits, stated plainly.** The securities memo is *market-valued* while the headline is face
value, so a rates move alone can produce a large Δsecurities against a small Δheadline — which is
almost certainly what the 2.98 ratio in the Q4 row is. That makes this test good at rejecting a
survey story (Q2's memo barely moves at all, in a quarter whose headline moves most) and poor at
attributing anything positively. And n=10 in the Q2 row is small; the p-value on the headline test
moves from 0.031 to 0.008 on a single exclusion, which is a warning about how much one observation
carries here.

### Leg 5 — what the print will actually say · **SUPPORTED**, and a number is registered

The 2027-03-31 release publishes the position **as-of 2026-12-31** on the series' one-quarter lag.
Three independent estimators, all built from first vintages only (a first vintage is what this event
prints, so a revised series would be the wrong base):

| Estimator | Base | Growth applied | Central |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year-over-year, same quarter | as-of 2025-12-31 first vintage **$29.448tn** (published 2026-03-31) | **+6.16%** (sd 0.90pp, **n=10**) — 2023Q1–2026Q1 excluding the 2024Q3 data event and its 2025Q3 mirror | **$31.26tn** |
| Q1→Q4, three quarters | as-of 2026-03-31 first vintage **$30.197tn** (published 2026-06-30) | **+4.66%** (sd 1.74pp, n=5) — 2021–2025 | **$31.60tn** |
| The sibling's own forecast, deflated | its registered **$32.01tn** for as-of 2027-03-31 | ÷ the Q4→Q1 seasonal **+2.01%** (sd 0.37pp, n=6, 2021+) | **$31.38tn** |

```
registered central = mean of the three  = $31.41tn
registered band                          = $30.7tn - $32.1tn
```

The band is the YoY estimator's ±2σ ($30.73–$31.79tn) widened at the top to cover the three-quarter
estimator's central and its own dispersion. The two excluded YoY observations are the same anomaly
seen twice: 2024Q3's bad first vintage prints **+0.58%** YoY and was restated **+$1,880.6bn** a
half-year later, and 2025Q3's comparison against that depressed base prints **+12.91%**. The
remaining ten run **4.27%–7.47%**.

**The coherence check is the finding here, not the point estimate.** Two ledgers, written by two
sessions, from two different bases, with two different estimators, now carry level forecasts one
quarter apart. Compounding this one forward by the Q4→Q1 seasonal gives **$31.89tn** against the
sibling's registered **$32.01tn** — **a 0.4% disagreement** on a $31tn series whose estimator sd is
0.9pp. Neither number is confirmed by the other, but they are not two independent guesses either;
they are two paths through the same archive arriving at the same place, and a future session that
kills one should expect to kill both.

### Leg 6 — does the release move the tape? · **REFUTED**, and the sibling's pooled result does not survive being split

The sibling left a standing instruction: *a matched control exists whenever a release recurs on the
same calendar anchor with a known treatment difference — use it instead of a synthetic one.* This
event has exactly that control available in the other direction: it is a **non**-revision edition,
and the sibling was the revision one.

Next-session \|Δ10Y\| on Treasury's daily par curve, first-vintage releases from 2012-01-01:

| Sample | n | mean \|Δ10Y\| | vs matched control | vs all sessions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **March editions** | **15** | **5.00bp** (sd 3.38, range 1–13bp) | t=+0.27, **p=0.78** | t=+1.27, **p=0.205** |
| The other three editions | 44 | 4.73bp (sd 3.17, range 0–12bp) | — | — |
| All sessions 2012–2026 | 3,671 | 3.89bp | — | — |

Two readings, and the second one is a correction to the record:

1. **The null is clean and the control is exact.** p=0.78. A March edition and any other edition of
   the same release are the same distribution. The March observations run **1bp to 13bp**, and the
   single 13bp (2024-03-29) was a quarter-end session that also carried a PCE print.
2. **The sibling's pooled p=0.041 does not reproduce on this sub-series.** Pooling all four editions
   against all sessions gave a publishable-looking result; **March alone against all sessions is
   p=0.205**. The sibling already read its own pooled number as the quarter-end calendar plus
   small-sample luck rather than a release effect, and splitting the series confirms that reading
   from the inside. Three ledgers have now examined last-business-day releases and none has found an
   effect that survives a matched control.

**No tape forward test is registered for this event, deliberately** — the same call the sibling made,
for the same reason. The corridor around 2027-03-31 is **12** tracked events in ±5 days, and several
own the tape outright: a `medium` **FTC v. Amazon antitrust trial** date on 03-29, `medium`
**consumer confidence** on 03-30, the **Good Friday** market closure on 03-26 truncating the week,
and two index-mechanics events (**Russell style capping**, **S&P select-sector secondary reweight**)
landing on 2027-03-31 itself. A next-session move on 2027-04-01 would be unattributable in advance,
and registering a test that cannot be scored honestly is worse than not registering one.

### What plays the conditions support

None. `symbols: []`, `low` tier, 16:00 ET publication after the equity cash close, reference data one
quarter old at print, **no accompanying press release** (Treasury states this on the release-dates
page — it is the ground on which [`tic-monthly-2027-02-18`](tic-monthly-2027-02-18.md) first declined

previously-anonymous member of a series a character, retires the intuitive reading of it, kills a
survey story on a second independent axis, and leaves a level forecast plus a *ranked* durability
prediction that a future session can score in ten minutes.
