## Initial research

### The question, plainly

Is 2026-11-12 real, what will the first Monthly Treasury Statement of FY2027 say, does it move
anything, and — since this is where the August sibling parked its market-impact forward test — is
that scoring date actually as clean as it was assumed to be?

**One-line verdict:** the date is primary-`confirmed` at 2:00 p.m. ET on two independent Treasury
sources and is **promoted from `estimate` this session**; the print itself is the same measured
non-event its sibling established, but its *content* is the first calendar-clean y/y deficit
comparison in a year and should show a genuine deterioration to roughly **$320B** (band $280–365B)
against **$284.35B** — and the "clean scoring date" premise behind FT-51 survives on the calendar
and is fragile on the volatility regime, which is a caveat worth registering 68 days early rather
than discovering at scoring.

### Conviction legs, tested

1. **The date is real, the time is 2:00 p.m. ET, and the entry is PROMOTED to `confirmed` —
   SUPPORTED by one Treasury primary read direct plus two independent corroborations.** The
   **November 2025 MTS**, page 40, downloaded and text-extracted this session, reads verbatim
   *"Listed below are the scheduled release dates for the Monthly Treasury Statement. The Statement
   is released at 2:00 p.m."* with **October 2026 → November 12, 2026**. Corroboration one: the
   **8th-federal-workday rule** — verified 20-of-20 against the published 2024 and 2026 calendars by
   the August sibling — computes November 2026's 8th workday as **Nov 12** exactly, once Veterans
   Day (Wed **Nov 11**) is excluded as a federal holiday. Corroboration two: Treasury's own
   **`services/calendar/release`** API carries a dataset (`015-BFS-2014Q1-13`) with exactly three
   forward releases — **2026-09-11, 2026-11-12, 2026-12-10** — matching the published table row for
   row *including the September gap*, at timestamps that decode to **14:00 ET on both sides of the
   DST boundary** (18:00 on 09-11, 19:00 on 11-12). That dataset is **not named** in any endpoint
   this session could reach (`static-data/datasets.json` returned 503, the id filter is ignored by
   the calendar endpoint), so it is offered as a fingerprint match, not as a named primary. The
   entry's original grounds for `estimate` — *"this lane's no-self-confirm limit on an event
   discovered in-sweep"* — are cleared: the discovery was the August sibling's, the primary has now
   been re-fetched independently by this event's own session, and `TSY:` is an authorized
   confirmed-tier prefix. The residual caveat, Treasury's own *"subject to change"*, applies equally
   to every already-`confirmed` Treasury date in this calendar and is not grounds to single this one
   out.

2. **Treasury's refusal to pre-date the fiscal-year-end statement is a standing rule, not a one-off
   — SUPPORTED, and this upgrades a sibling's observation.** The August ledger's 2026-09-05 pulse
   found the September-2026 row carrying footnote (1), *"Release date subject to completion of
   year-end reporting requirements"*, and corrected a phantom "2026-10-14 September-data edition"
   out of its own doc. This session fetched the **November 2024** edition and found the identical
   footnote on the **September 2025** row of the prior year's calendar. So the gap is Treasury's
   standing practice for the FY-end statement, and the release-calendar API agrees from the other
   side — dataset `015-BFS-2014Q1-13` has **no October 2026 entry at all**. Consequence for this
   ledger: as of today, **2026-11-12 is the next dated MTS**, and whether the FY2026 final statement
   (which historically lands in October and would carry the full-year deficit) publishes before it
   is genuinely unscheduled. That is a watch item for the D-10 pulse, not an assumption.

3. **October 2026 and October 2025 both carry two benefit batches, so this is the calendar-clean
   comparison — SUPPORTED, and it is the load-bearing structural finding.** The payment rule
   Treasury states in its own Highlight boxes is that the military/VA/SSI/Medicare-drug batch nominally
   paid on the 1st moves to the prior business day when the 1st is a weekend or holiday. Applied to
   October, two dates matter — whether Oct 1 falls in the month (its own batch) and whether Nov 1
   falls on a weekend (the next month's batch pulled back):

   | Fiscal year | Oct 1 | Nov 1 | Batches in October | Five DTS benefit lines, $B | MTS October deficit, $B |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | FY2023 (Oct 2022) | **Sat** | Tue | **0** | **1.7** | 87.8 |
   | FY2024 (Oct 2023) | **Sun** | Wed | **0** | **6.3** | 66.6 |
   | FY2025 (Oct 2024) | Tue | Fri | **1** | **45.5** | 257.5 |
   | FY2026 (Oct 2025) | Wed | **Sat** | **2** | **108.5** | 284.35 |
   | FY2027 (Oct 2026) | Thu | **Sun** | **2** | *predicted 100–125* | *this print* |

   The five lines are Medicare Prescription Drugs, VA Benefits, DoD Military Active Duty Pay, DoD
   Military Retirement and SSA Supplemental Security Income, summed from daily DTS withdrawals. The
   batch count predicts the total with no fitting: 0 batches → $1.7B and $6.3B; 1 → $45.5B; 2 →
   $108.5B. **October 2026 matches October 2025 exactly**, which is what makes this print readable
   where August's was not — August 2026 carried zero batches against two a year earlier, a $74.1B
   swing before anything economic happened.

4. **FY2026 is deteriorating at +10.5% y/y, not +1.3% — SUPPORTED, and it corrects a sibling's
   arithmetic.** MTS Table 1 prints the prior fiscal year **month by month for all twelve months**
   and its prior-year "Year-to-Date" line is therefore the **full** prior year, not the comparable
   period. Re-summing the FY2025 block month by month for the same ten months FY2026 has published:

   | Ten months, Oct–Jul | FY2025 | FY2026 | Change |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Receipts | $4,346.6B | $4,485.4B | **+3.19%** |
   | Outlays | $5,975.2B | $6,284.2B | **+5.17%** |
   | Deficit | $1,628.5B | $1,798.8B | **+10.46%** |

   The August sibling's leg 8 compared FY2026's ten months against **$1,775.4B**, which this session
   confirms is FY2025's **full twelve-month** total (the twelve months sum to it exactly), and
   reported **+1.3%**. The correct comparable is **+10.5%**. This is recorded here rather than
   edited into that ledger — rows are append-only and another event owns it — and it *strengthens*
   its conclusion rather than undermining it: FY2026 is running materially worse than FY2025, so its
   "the August improvement is mechanical" reading was, if anything, understated.

5. **Every dollar of FY2026's receipts growth is tariffs, and the tariff line is now shrinking —
   SUPPORTED, and this is the leg the October forecast rests on.** Summing the DTS customs deposit
   line (renamed mid-series from *"DHS – Customs and Certain Excise Taxes"* to *"DHS – Customs
   Duties, Taxes, and Fees"*) over the same ten months: **FY2025 $152.2B → FY2026 $281.0B**, a
   +$128.8B increase against a total receipts increase of +$138.8B. Strip it out and **ex-customs
   receipts grew from $4,194.4B to $4,204.5B — +0.24%**, flat in nominal terms and falling in real
   ones. The monthly customs line has since rolled over:

   | | Oct-25 | Nov | Dec | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug-26 |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | Customs receipts, $B | **34.2** | 32.9 | 30.4 | 30.5 | 28.6 | 25.1 | 24.8 | 23.5 | 24.8 | 26.1 | **25.0** |

   The step down tracks the IEEPA strike-down (SCOTUS 6–3, 2026-02-20; collection stopped 02-24) and
   the substitution into Section 122 and then Section 301 on 2026-07-24 — a sequence sourced from
   the [tariff-truce ledger](us-china-tariff-truce-expiry-2026-11-10.md), press-based, and labeled
   as such. What is primary is the receipts line itself, and it says October 2026 should collect
   roughly **$25B** where October 2025 collected **$34.2B** — a **−$9B** y/y receipts headwind before
   any economics.

6. **Base case: a deficit near $320B, honest band $280–365B — SUPPORTED as a model, with the width
   stated rather than hidden.** Two builds, both anchored on the batch-matched prior October
   (receipts **$404.37B**, outlays **$688.72B**, deficit **$284.35B** as printed; restated $284.33B):

   - **Component build.** Receipts = ex-customs $370.2B grown at the measured +0.24%–3% → $371–381B,
     plus customs $23–27B → **$393–408B**. Outlays = $688.7B at the measured +5.17% → ~$724B, plus a
     CBP refund tail of roughly $3–12B against October 2025's $1.9B → **$710–745B**. Deficit
     **$302–352B**, central ~$327B.
   - **Deficit-growth build.** $284.35B × the measured FY2026 deficit growth of +10.46% → **$314B**.
     This one understates by construction: it assumes FY2026's tariff-boosted receipts mix repeats,
     and leg 5 shows it does not.

   Taking the component build as primary and the growth build as a floor check gives a central
   estimate of **~$320B** and a band of **$280–365B**. The band is wide **because October has not
   started**: the sibling's DTS→MTS mapping (mean bias +$30.6B, σ $31.9B) needs a closed month and
   cannot be run until ~2026-11-02. It is also the wrong constant for October — the DTS ex-debt gap
   ran **$70.5B → $87.8B (+17.3)** in Oct-22, **$29.0B → $66.6B (+37.6)** in Oct-23, **$196.8B →
   $257.5B (+60.7)** in Oct-24 and **$215.1B → $284.3B (+69.2)** in Oct-25. October's bias is
   systematically larger than the pooled figure and trending up; on n=4, the D-10 pulse should use
   **+$60–70B**, not +$30.6B, and should say so when it does.

7. **It does not move anything — SUPPORTED, inherited, and not re-measured.** The August sibling

8. **The FT-51 scoring date is clean of events and not clean of regime — SUPPORTED, and this is the

   | 17–20 | 155 | 0.203% | 3 (**1.9%**) |

   The σ is **flat at ~0.20% right up to VIX 20** and then jumps ~80%. So ±0.60% is a ~3σ threshold

### Honest limits

The **$320B central estimate is a forecast, not a mapping** — October 2026 has not begun, so unlike
the August sibling (which computed its number from a closed month's daily data to ±$32B) this leans
on ten-month growth rates projected onto a single volatile month. October is the most
calendar-distorted month in the MTS year, and the batch-count model in leg 3, though it reproduces
four Octobers with no fitting, rests on **n=4**. The ±$42B band should be read as the honest width
of a D-68 estimate and is expected to halve at D-10, not as a claim to precision. The **customs
projection assumes Section 301 collections hold near their August run-rate**; a court challenge to
the Section 301 substitution, or a China snapback on 11-10 that changes trade volumes, would move
that line and neither is modeled. The **IEEPA/Section 122/Section 301 sequence and the ~$165B refund
order remain press- and law-firm-sourced**; cbo.gov still could not be read (it serves a JS
challenge to `curl` and 403s WebFetch), so CBO's Monthly Budget Review — which publishes *ahead* of
the MTS and would corroborate the refund figures — is again undatable and is deliberately **not**
proposed as a calendar entry on a guessed date. The **release-calendar API fingerprint in leg 1 is
unnamed**: three dates and a DST-consistent 14:00 ET clock matching the published table is strong
circumstantial corroboration, but this session could not resolve `015-BFS-2014Q1-13` to the string
"Monthly Treasury Statement" on any reachable endpoint, and it is not counted as a second named
primary. The **market-impact null is inherited, not re-measured**, and the vol-regime study in leg 8
is unconditional — it measures what an ordinary 13:30→close window does at a given VIX, not what
MTS days specifically do, because splitting 35 release windows by VIX bucket would not be powered.
No options-implied or MOVE-based test was run.
