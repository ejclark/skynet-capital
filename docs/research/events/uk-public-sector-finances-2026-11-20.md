## At a glance

**TL;DR.** **Three sibling lanes proposed this print as "the first outturn scored against the new
Budget-day OBR forecast." Two ONS primaries, fetched directly today, say it will not be — and the
reason is a lag nobody here had measured.** The ONS does not re-benchmark on Budget day; it
re-benchmarks when the OBR's **monthly profiles** land, roughly five weeks after the forecast itself.
Its own two most recent rounds are almost identical: Autumn Budget **2024-10-30** → first bulletin
naming the new basis published **2025-01-22** (**D+84**); Autumn Budget **2025-11-26** → the ONS states
verbatim that the new profiles *"will be used to inform our Public Sector Finances, UK January 2026
statistical bulletin on 20 February 2026"* (**D+86**). 2026-10-28 + 85 days is **2027-01-21** — about
**three prints after this one**. So on **2026-11-20** the *"versus forecast"* line is still the
**March 2026** profile, the same superseded one the 10-21 print used. What this print genuinely is
instead: the **first outturn whose reference month contains Budget day** (measures in the numerator, a
pre-Budget profile in the denominator) and the **first VAT month** carrying the SI 2026/987 electricity
zero-rate. The proposals also missed the collision that does exist — **2026-11-20 is US monthly options
expiration** — though measurement drains that too: **42 of 137** PSF release days since 2015-04-23
(**30.7%**) already fall on an opex Friday, and *both* day-types are **quieter** than average. The
market half is a six-way null on double the sibling's sample: IGLT.L **1.07x** an ordinary day
(**t = 0.70**), FTSE **0.83x** (**t = -2.30**). `symbols: []`, no house playbook is fiscal-, rates- or

## Initial research

### The question, plainly

This event reached the calendar as **three proposals and no canonical file** — from
[`uk-cpi-2026-11-18`](uk-cpi-2026-11-18.md), [`uk-labour-market-2026-11-17`](uk-labour-market-2026-11-17.md)
and [`uk-public-sector-finances-2026-10-21`](uk-public-sector-finances-2026-10-21.md), all filed
2026-09-09. All three were read in full before anything here was written, and they converge on one
substantive claim, stated most sharply by the third:

> *"2026-11-20 is the first print whose 'versus forecast' line is scored against the Budget-day EFO
> rather than a superseded one, and therefore the first honest read on whether the Chancellor's
> arithmetic survived contact with an outturn."*

and one mechanics claim, stated most sharply by the second: that ONS **splits** CPI and PSF onto
separate mornings in November (unlike the 2026-10-21 same-minute co-print), so *"a calendar that
carries the CPI half alone would leave a second pre-dawn UK release invisible two days later."*

**One-line verdict:** the mechanics claim is **right but incomplete** — the pre-dawn window is clean and
the *session* is not, because 11-20 is monthly opex — and the substance claim is **refuted**: the ONS
re-benchmarks when the OBR's monthly **profiles** publish, not when the Budget lands, and its own two
most recent rounds put that at **D+84** and **D+86**, so this print at **D+23** is still scored against
**March 2026**.

**Method.** Sourced web research per [`EVENT-RESEARCH.md`](../../process/EVENT-RESEARCH.md), plus a
first-party event study against this repo's own market-data layer
(`scripts/research/market-data.mjs`, split/dividend-adjusted daily bars). Primaries fetched direct
2026-09-09, all HTTP 200: the ONS release-calendar entries for public sector finances **October 2026**
and **November 2026**; the ONS release-calendar entry for consumer price inflation **October 2026**; the
ONS **latest** PSF bulletin (July 2026 data, published 2026-08-21); and the ONS PSF bulletins for
**October 2024**, **November 2024**, **December 2024** and **November 2025** editions — the four that
bracket the two most recent Autumn Budgets. The full release-date series came from the ONS's **own**
bulletin index via `api.beta.ons.gov.uk` (**137** editions, 2015-04-23 → 2026-08-21), after the HTML
`previousReleases` page **404**'d; both the failure and the substitute are recorded in

`symbols: []`, no issuer, and neither has a macro mode; the cache was busted regardless, per this

### Conviction legs, tested

**1. The date, the time and the designation are all publisher primaries from one page.** **SUPPORTED.**
`ons.gov.uk/releases/publicsectorfinancesukoctober2026` reads *"Public sector finances, UK: October
2026"*, *"20 November 2026 at 7:00am"*, contents *"the headline measures of Public Sector Finance
Statistics (net borrowing net cash requirement and net debt)"*, marked *"This release is not yet
published"* — and the same page carries the Office for Statistics Regulation accreditation statement,
so unlike the sibling ledger the designation is read from the release page rather than inherited from a
bulletin. Cadence corroborates twice over: **137** consecutive PSF bulletins from 2015-04-23 to
2026-08-21 all fall on the **19th–26th**, and all **11** October-edition bulletins — 2015-11-20,
2016-11-22, 2017-11-21, 2018-11-21, 2019-11-21, 2020-11-20, 2021-11-19, 2022-11-22, 2023-11-21,
2024-11-21, 2025-11-21 — fall on **19–22 November**. The `estimate` label is the taxonomy gap and the
no-self-confirm limit, not a doubt.

**2. The premise all three proposals share is refuted: the ONS re-benchmarks on the OBR's monthly
profiles, not on Budget day.** **REFUTED**, and it is this document's central finding. The mechanism is
visible in the live bulletin itself: it says its comparisons use the *"Economic and fiscal outlook –
March 2026 report"*, published **3 March 2026**, *"with corresponding monthly profiles published in
**April 2026**"* — a roughly five-week gap between a forecast and the profiles that make it usable
month by month. Two rounds show what that gap does to the first post-Budget bulletin, and they agree to
within two days:

| Round | Budget / EFO | Bulletin at ~D+23 | First bulletin on the new basis | Lag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2024 | **2024-10-30** | published **2024-11-21**: *"The statistics in this bulletin do not yet fully reflect these updated forecasts"* (the 2024-12-20 one repeats it) | published **2025-01-22**: *"This chart uses forecast data published in the OBR's Economic and fiscal outlook – October 2024 and monthly profiles published in December 2024."* | **D+84** |
| 2025 | **2025-11-26** | published **2025-12-19**: *"We expect corresponding monthly profiles to be published in January 2026"* | ONS names it in advance: *"New monthly receipts profiles consistent with the OBR's Autumn 2025 Budget will be used to inform our Public Sector Finances, UK January 2026 statistical bulletin on **20 February 2026**."* | **D+86** |

The 2025 row is the exact structural analogue — a bulletin published **D+23** after an Autumn Budget,
the same offset as this print — and it says the new profiles are three publications away. Projected
forward, **2026-10-28 + 85 days ≈ 2027-01-21**, which by cadence is the **December-2026-data** bulletin,
about **three prints after this one**. So the *"versus forecast"* line on 2026-11-20 will still read
against **March 2026**. Registered as **FT-…-1**, and graded **high** rather than medium for a reason
worth stating: every figure above was fetched **directly from ons.gov.uk**, with no `obr.uk` page and
no search relay anywhere in the chain.

**3. The correction is not a downgrade — it names a sharper hazard than the one it replaces.**
**SUPPORTED**, by arithmetic on the publisher's own definitions. The reference month is **October
2026**, which **contains Budget day (2026-10-28)**. So any Budget measure with immediate or near-immediate
effect begins landing in the outturn, while the profile the outturn is measured against was built in
**March 2026** and knows nothing about those measures. That is an asymmetry, not a comparison: the
*"versus forecast"* gap on 11-20 can widen or narrow for a reason that is neither fiscal drift nor
forecast error, and a reader who treats it as either has been misled by a line the bulletin prints in
good faith. This is a strictly worse trap than the one the 10-21 lane wrote about, because a co-print is
visible on the calendar and this is only visible in the definitions.

**4. The other thing October 2026 is: the first VAT month of the electricity zero-rate, and the first
clean month after the summer VAT cut.** **MIXED**, and graded down on recording basis rather than on
sourcing. Two policy effects sit in this one reference month. The
[`uk-electricity-vat-zero-rate-2026-10-01`](uk-electricity-vat-zero-rate-2026-10-01.md) lane established
from `legislation.gov.uk` that **SI 2026/987** zero-rates domestic electricity in Great Britain from
**1 October 2026** to **31 March 2027**; and the live ONS bulletin records that the **Great British
Summer Savings scheme** temporarily reduced VAT on eligible family activities *"from 25 July to 1
September 2026"*, which the government *"estimated … will cost around £300 million in total."* October
is therefore the first month with the zero-rate on and the summer scheme off. **The reason this is MIXED
and not SUPPORTED:** VAT enters the public sector finances on a **time-shifted accrual** basis, so an
October reference month captures only part of October's liability, and the ONS publishes no
electricity-only VAT line at all. This print is where the zero-rate's cost **starts** appearing — it is
not where it is measured. The proposing `uk-cpi-2026-11-18` lane's framing of this as *"the first
INDEPENDENT measurement"* of the SI's Exchequer cost is too strong for that reason, and is amended here
rather than repeated. It is also, per that lane, a **six-month** notch that sunsets **2027-03-31** from
inside the Order — so a widening is not a structural deterioration.

**7. The mechanics claim is right about the morning and silent about the day — 11-20 is monthly opex.**
**MIXED**, and this is the amendment to the proposals' second premise. The split is real and
publisher-verified: `ons.gov.uk/releases/consumerpriceinflationukoctober2026` reads *"Consumer price
inflation, UK: October 2026"*, *"18 November 2026 7:00am"*, not yet published — two days before this
release, not the same minute. So the pre-dawn window on 11-20 has **one** candidate UK explanation where
10-21 had two. But all three proposals stop there, and the calendar says the day is not clean:
[`opex-2026-11-20`](opex-2026-11-20.md) is **confirmed**, `medium`, the standard OCC third-Friday
monthly, and that ledger measures the surrounding corridor as the **second-densest of the four opex
cycles in this batch** (28 events within ±5 days, impact-weight 43). **Two measurements keep this from
being over-read.** First, the overlap is **ordinary**: **42 of 137** PSF release days since 2015-04-23
(**30.7%**) already fell on a monthly opex Friday, a mechanical consequence of PSF sitting on the
19th–26th and the third Friday on the 15th–21st — unlike the CPI co-print, which was 1-in-30. Second,
the direction is the **opposite** of intuition: opex Fridays are themselves *quieter* than ordinary
sessions (^GSPC **0.90x**, ^VIX **0.92x**, ^FTSE **0.90x**, ^TNX **0.85x** with t = **-2.08**),
consistent with the gamma-pin compression the opex ledger documents. Isolating further — opex days
**with** a PSF print against opex days **without** one — gives IGLT.L 1.27x (t = 1.19), ^VIX 1.42x
(t = 1.29), ^GSPC 1.20x (t = 0.80): nothing significant, on n = 42 vs 91. **The honest statement is
therefore a reading rule, not a warning:** 11-20 is a doubly-quiet day by construction, and a US-session
move on it belongs to opex and its corridor, never to a 02:00 ET foreign borrowing print.

come out.** **REFUTED**, and it is worth recording because it is the obvious hypothesis this event
invites. Taking every PSF print landing **15–45 days after** a UK fiscal event (Budget, Autumn
Statement, Spring Statement) since 2015 — **n = 19**, including the exact-offset analogues 2018-11-21

post-Budget premium is claimed here.

### Honest limits

**First, the central finding is a two-round convention, not a rule.** D+84 and D+86 agreeing to two days
is striking, but two rounds is two rounds. The ONS has never published a *policy* saying when it
re-benchmarks; what it has published is two instances plus the mechanism (EFO → profiles ≈ five weeks)
that explains them. A round where the OBR publishes profiles unusually fast would break the projection
without breaking the mechanism. That is precisely why leg 2 is registered as **FT-…-1** rather than
asserted — but note it is graded **high** where the sibling's analogous finding was **medium**, because
every source here is a direct ons.gov.uk fetch and `obr.uk` appears nowhere in the chain.

**Second, one of the four bulletin fetches is thinner than the others.** The **2024-11-21** bulletin
yielded *"The statistics in this bulletin do not yet fully reflect these updated forecasts"* but **no**

**Third, the VAT leg is a recording-basis limit, not a sourcing one.** The legislation and the summer
scheme are both primaries. What is *not* established is that an October reference month captures the
zero-rate's monthly cost — time-shifted accrual and the absence of an electricity-only VAT line mean it
does not, and the proposing lane's *"first INDEPENDENT measurement"* framing is amended in leg 4 rather

**Fourth, the market study's limits, which are the sibling's and one more.** IGLT.L is an
**all-maturity** gilt index, so it under-weights the long end where UK fiscal stress shows; it stops

bars against IGLT.L's 137 and unreliable close timing; its row is reported for completeness and carries
no weight. The one *new* limit: leg 6's headline slice is **n = 11**, which is a description of eleven
mornings, not an estimate of a distribution — the ±0.81% threshold in **FT-…-2** is deliberately taken
from the **full-sample p90** (2,874 sessions) rather than from those eleven.

**Fifth, this release has no statutory-register corroboration.** The gov.uk pre-announcement slug

## Stance & kill switches

**Stance (2026-09-09, D-72; date `estimate`): stand aside completely, and spend the run-in correcting
the frame all three proposals filed this event under rather than forecasting a number nobody here can
trade.** This book holds nothing with a fiscal, gilt or sterling channel and none is proposed; the `low`
tier is **measured** in leg 5 on 137 release days, not inherited. Three positions, all analytical.

**First, the shared substance premise is refuted, and that is this document's title.** All three
proposals filed this as the first outturn scored against the Budget-day EFO. The ONS's own two most
recent rounds say it re-benchmarks when the OBR's **monthly profiles** publish — **D+84** after the
2024-10-30 Budget (first use in the bulletin published 2025-01-22, which names *"monthly profiles
published in December 2024"*) and **D+86** after the 2025-11-26 Budget (the ONS naming *"20 February
2026"* in advance) — and the mechanism behind both is visible in the live bulletin, which pairs the
*"March 2026 report"* with *"corresponding monthly profiles published in April 2026."* At **D+23**, this
print is still on the **March 2026** profile. Unlike the sibling ledger's structurally identical
correction, this one rests entirely on **direct ons.gov.uk fetches** — no obr.uk page, no search relay —
so it is registered at **high** confidence.

**Second, the print is more interesting than the frame it loses, for two reasons.** Its reference month
**contains Budget day**, so Budget measures land in the outturn while the profile scoring them predates
the Budget — an asymmetry that makes the *"versus forecast"* headline actively misleading rather than
merely stale. And October 2026 is the first VAT month with the **SI 2026/987** electricity zero-rate on
and the **Great British Summer Savings** scheme off; that is where the zero-rate's cost begins to
appear, though time-shifted accrual means it is not where it is measured.

**Third, the mechanics premise is right about the morning and incomplete about the day.** ONS does split
CPI (**2026-11-18**, publisher-verified) from PSF (**2026-11-20**), so the pre-dawn window has one
candidate explanation where 10-21 had two. But **2026-11-20 is US monthly opex** (confirmed), which all
three proposals missed. Measurement drains the alarm from that: the overlap is **ordinary** (42 of 137
PSF days, 30.7%, against a 1-in-30 CPI co-print) and both day-types are **quieter** than average
(PSF-day FTSE 0.83x, t = -2.30; opex-Friday ^TNX 0.85x, t = -2.08). So the correct output is an
attribution rule pointing the other way from October's: pre-dawn may be the ONS, the US session never is.
