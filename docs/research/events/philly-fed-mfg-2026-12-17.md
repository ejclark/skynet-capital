## Initial research

### The question, plainly

The proposal that filed this id made a claim and then said it could not check it: that the
Philadelphia Fed's December collection window — unlike Empire's — actually spans the **2026-12-11**
funding cliff, making this "the pair's half whose collection window actually spans the 12-11
cliff." It assigned the writer of the canonical file two jobs: **(a)** re-verify the date, which it
could only reproduce by the third-Thursday rule, and **(b)** read the bank's actual collection
window rather than inherit a sibling's inference. So: is the date right, is the window claim right,
and if it is, does any of it change what the 2026-12-17 08:30 ET print is worth?

**One-line verdict:** both jobs discharge from the primary, the date is now read rather than
derived, and the window claim **survives at about a fifth of its advertised size** — three of eight
calendar days but only **one of six business days** sits post-cliff — while a better-powered
version of the sibling's own lapse test (13 windows, not 4) says a lapse leaves no measurable mark
on this survey regardless; what genuinely changes on 12-17 is not the print but the **morning**,
which inverts from unreadable to solitary.

### What the conditions support

Nothing directional, and less than the proposal implied. What the conditions support is a **reading
discipline with three measured foundations.** First, a **corrected framing of what this print is**:
its collection window really does reach past the cliff — by one business day out of six, on a rule

### Honest limits
