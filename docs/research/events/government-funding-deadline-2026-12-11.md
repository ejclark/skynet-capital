### The question, plainly

The September cliff was averted on 2026-09-01. This entry is what it handed off to. Three things need
answering: **is December 11 actually the date** (i.e. was the CR enacted, and is the Dec 4 branch
dead), **is a December lapse more or less likely than the September one was**, and — the part the

### Conviction legs, tested

1. **The Dec 4 branch is dead and Dec 11 is the operative date — SUPPORTED.** The House did not amend;
   it **adopted the Senate's version** of H.R. 6500 on **2026-09-01** by **370–48** (R 193–19,
   D 176–29), under suspension of the rules, which is why a two-thirds threshold was in play. The
   Senate had passed it **90–6** on **2026-08-08**. govinfo's bill summary states the operative
   mechanic precisely: continuing FY2027 appropriations at FY2026 levels **"through the earlier of
   December 11, 2026, or the enactment of the applicable appropriations act."** That "earlier of"
   clause matters and is not decorative — the cliff is **per-bill**, so a partial full-year package in
   the lame duck carves agencies out of it one at a time rather than all-or-nothing.

2. **Enactment could not be verified, and the veto-proof margins are why that does not undermine the
   date — SUPPORTED, with the verification failure stated plainly.** Every outlet reached on 2026-09-02
   said the President was **"expected to sign it in short order"**; none reported a signing.
   congress.gov and govtrack both returned **403**; govinfo's BILLSUM record still showed latest action
   **"Passed Senate, 2026-08-08"**; CRFB's tracker was last updated **2026-08-12**. So there is no
   primary record of enactment, and this entry stays `estimate` on that ground alone (it may not
   self-confirm from this lane regardless). **But the arithmetic bounds the risk:** 370 of 418 votes
   cast is above the 279 a House override needs, and 90 of 96 is above 64 in the Senate. A veto is
   therefore not a live path to changing this date; the realistic failure mode is a *delay* in
   signature, which would revive the **09-30** cliff, not move 12-11.

3. **This cliff is harder than September's, and the reason is that September's protection was
   explicitly temporary — SUPPORTED, and it is the load-bearing finding.** The 09-30 sibling's own
   winning argument was that the Collins–Murray deal existed to fund **past the November 2026
   midterms** so neither party owned a pre-election shutdown. That argument is a clock, and it runs out
   on **2026-11-03**. Everything it was suppressing — levels, riders, the OMB grant-rule fight the CR
   only *pauses* "while the CR is in place," the Border Patrol transfer restriction — comes back in a
   session where the electoral cost has already been paid. Brownstein (2026-08-08) names the specific
   aggravator: *"If Democrats gain control of either chamber in November, Republicans are likely to
   intensify efforts to secure action on key policy priorities before the new Congress is sworn in,
   adding further complexity to year-end funding negotiations."*

4. **The underlying appropriations work is not close to done — SUPPORTED, and it is the measurable
   half of leg 3.** CRFB's FY2027 tracker (last updated **2026-08-12**): of twelve bills, **3** have
   passed the House floor (Agriculture, MilCon-VA, National Security-State), **0** have passed the
   Senate floor, and **0** are enacted. That is the same posture that made a CR inevitable in
   September, now pointed at a deadline with **roughly five session weeks** after the midterms and a
   live NDAA competing for the same floor time. The honest reading is that the lame duck's realistic
   menu is an omnibus, a partial "minibus" plus a CR for the rest, or another CR — **not** twelve
   enacted bills — and each of those is a negotiation that can fail on a date.

5. **The calendar mechanics cut the other way, and this is the strongest mitigant — SUPPORTED.**
   **2026-12-11 is a Friday.** Funding lapses at 00:00 on **Saturday 2026-12-12**; no federal employee
   misses a work day until **Monday 2026-12-14**, and no market session opens inside the lapse until
   then either. Congress gets a weekend of grace it did not have on a Wednesday deadline. The 2026
   precedent is directly on point and points *toward* survivability rather than away from it: the
   **Jan 30 – Feb 3, 2026** partial shutdown was itself a weekend lapse and is remembered as a
   scheduling artifact, not an economic event. A December date adds a second, softer mitigant — a
   holiday-season shutdown is unusually expensive to own politically.

## Stance & kill switches

**Stance (date effectively certain, event conditional, entry filed `estimate` — enactment unverified
as of 2026-09-02).** Take **no position, no hedge and no sizing change** off this event. On the date:
**High** confidence that 2026-12-11 is the operative cliff — the House adopted the Senate's text
unamended and both margins are veto-proof, so the only realistic date risk is a signature delay, which

September one was, because the restraint that produced 90–6 was explicitly pre-election and expires on
11-03, while the underlying work sits at 0 of 12 enacted and 0 of 12 through the Senate floor. Two
structural mitigants cut against that: a **Friday** deadline means the lapse begins on a weekend with
no furlough day until Monday 12-14, and a holiday-season shutdown is expensive to own.

**The stance's actual content is the exposure map, not the odds.** In the lapse branch this calendar
keeps **jobs 12-04, the 12-09 FOMC, CPI 12-10, opex 12-18 and the 12-30 minutes**, and loses **PPI
12-15, import/export prices 12-17, PCE 12-23 and every January release** into the **2027-01-27** FOMC.
That is the inverse of the September case — the dots *do* exist this time, and what disappears is every
subsequent check on them. It is also a correction to this event's own calendar note, which lists only
PCE 12-23. Equity base rates (median 0.0% during 22 gaps since 1976) are explicitly **not** the reason
to watch this.
