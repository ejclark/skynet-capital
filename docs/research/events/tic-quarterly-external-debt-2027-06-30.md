## At a glance

fetched the whole archive and found the claim is harder than anyone argued — and that its natural
follow-on is false.** Treasury publishes gross external debt four times a year, each on a quarter's
last business day, and says revisions "*are normally released on the last business day of June*."
All **135** archived Table A vintages were pulled from `ticdata.treasury.gov` and each one's own
`Source: Department of the Treasury, <date>` stamp read. Of **66** revisions to an
already-published quarter, **66 carry a June stamp**. Zero March, zero September, zero December,
**2012 through 2026**. The June edition is not the *largest* revision of the year — it is the

## Initial research

### The question

[`tic-annual-survey-prelim-2027-02-26`](tic-annual-survey-prelim-2027-02-26.md) proved that the
annual survey benchmark cannot reach the February monthly vintage and is undocumented for March,
and relocated it here on one sentence of Treasury prose. Two questions follow. Is the relocation
right? And if it is, is the survey actually what makes this release's revision large — or did the
calendar just move an unexamined assumption from one date to another?

### One-line verdict

The relocation is **right and much harder than it was filed** — June is not the biggest revision
edition, it is the *only* one, 66 for 66 — and the assumption that travelled with it is **wrong**:
the survey-sensitive component explains **2%** of the revision it was supposed to cause.

### Method

Sourced web research per [`EVENT-RESEARCH.md`](../../process/EVENT-RESEARCH.md) (macro-print mode
— no symbol instruments exist for this kind; `symbols: []`). Primaries fetched **direct** this
session (2026-09-09, plain curl, HTTP 200): Treasury's TIC release-dates table (**111,181 bytes**),
including its full REVISIONS-and-RELEASE-DATES note block; Treasury's *U.S. Gross External Debt*
statistics page (**174,128 bytes**), which carries a per-as-of-quarter index of **every** current

from the index page's labels. Last-business-day arithmetic was recomputed from `datetime` with
5 U.S.C. 6103 holidays constructed from the statute's rules rather than a list.
`probe-ref.blocked` is empty: no cited source failed.

### Leg 1 — the date · **SUPPORTED**, and promoted `estimate` → `confirmed`

Treasury's 2027 table puts June's row at **30** in the column headed *Quarterly: U.S. gross
external debt at end of previous quarter*, under *"All data releases occur at 4 p.m. Washington,
D.C. time"*. The note block states the rule in its own words: *"The release dates are the last
business day of each quarter (March, June, September, and December)."* **2027-06-30 is a Wednesday
and the last business day of June 2027.**

A second Treasury primary, fetched the same session, states the same rule independently — and adds
a detail the release-dates page omits:

> *"Releases are made at 4 PM in Washington D.C. on the last business day of March, June, and
> September, and at 10 AM on the last business day of December."*

The mechanical corroboration here is **empirical rather than arithmetic**, and that is what makes
it worth more than the sibling's. Every archived vintage carries its own publication stamp, so the
rule can be checked against **65 dated publications** (2011-2026) instead of against a schedule
table's own future rows:

| | n | at offset +0 |
|---|---|---|
| All scheduled publications 2011-2026 | 65 | **55** (57 within ±1 day) |
| **June editions only** | **14** | **13** (the 14th at +2) |

The four genuine exceptions are the finding, not the noise:

| Published | Scheduled | Offset | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019-01-31 | 2018-12-31 | **+31** | the 35-day funding lapse (2018-12-22 → 2019-01-25) |
| 2024-12-30 | 2024-12-31 | **−1** | published a day early |
| 2026-01-16 | 2025-12-31 | **+16** | flagged **"16 (delayed)"** on the release-dates page itself |
| 2025-07-02 | 2025-06-30 | **+2** | the only June slip in the series |

**Three of the four are December releases** — the same release the statistics page singles out as
the 10 AM one. Whatever operational difference that time reflects, the slip record tracks it. June
is the reliable quarter, and this event is a June event.

Promotion follows [`tic-annual-survey-prelim-2027-02-26`](tic-annual-survey-prelim-2027-02-26.md)
and [`mts-october-2026-11-12`](mts-october-2026-11-12.md): one named Treasury primary plus
independent mechanical corroboration. The `estimate` ground the proposal cited was this lane's
**no-self-confirm** limit — the entry was discovered by the annual-survey sweep, not researched.
That is cleared here by this event's own session re-fetching both pages. As on every sibling,
`confirmed` describes **the published schedule and nothing else**; the release-dates page carries
the standing notice that a date *"will be revised after offices reopen"* if Washington D.C. offices
are closed — and the 2019 exception above is exactly that notice firing.

### Leg 2 — is June really where the revisions are? · **SUPPORTED, and far more strongly than proposed**

The sibling relocated the benchmark here on one sentence of prose. This is testable, because
Treasury's statistics page indexes **every archived vintage** of every table it has ever published,
and each vintage stamps its own release date inside the file.

All 135 Table A vintages were fetched and stamped. Grouping every revision — every case where an
as-of quarter has a later vintage than the one before it — by the **release month of the new
vintage**:

```
revisions by release month, all archived vintages 2011-2026
  June       66
  March       0
  September   0
  December    0
```

**Sixty-six for sixty-six.** In fifteen years, no March, September or December release of this
series has ever restated a previously published quarter. And the June edition's scope is equally
regular — it restates the **four quarters of the previous calendar year**, every year:

Mean revision per quarter, as a share of the level: **1.015%** (median 1.066%, sd 0.538, range

The **2024Q3** row deserves its own sentence, because it is not a benchmark revision at all. Its
first vintage printed **$25.798tn** on **2024-12-30** — a fall of $1.1tn from 2024Q2's $26.896tn,
in a series that had never fallen more than 0.3% — and the June-2025 edition restated it to
**$27.679tn**. That is a data-quality event corrected a half-year later, and it is the single
largest observation in the panel. It published on a December release, one day early. Any forecast
built on this series has to carry the possibility of another one; the registered band in leg 4
does.

**What this does to the sibling's claim.** The sibling wrote that June is the survey's *"one
currently-documented destination"* and asked the calendar to watch it. That is now understated:
June is the *only* destination for **any** revision to this series, documented or not. The
relocation was right, and the evidence for it is a census rather than a reading of tense.

### Leg 3 — is the survey what makes the June revision big? · **REFUTED**

This is the assumption that travelled with the relocation and was never tested. Treasury's note
says the June revisions *"include the results of the Survey of Foreign Holdings of U.S.
Securities."* "Include" is not "consist of," and Table A carries the discriminator in its own
memorandum block: **Debt Securities: By Sector**, the market-valued total of debt securities
inside the external debt position. That line is what a securities survey can move. Loans,
currency and deposits, trade credit and intercompany lending come from the banking and
direct-investment forms, and the survey has nothing to say about them.

Across the **44** BPM6 vintage pairs where the memorandum item is readable (2015Q1 → 2025Q4; the
2014Q4 pair is excluded because the BPM5→BPM6 format change makes its memo item non-comparable):

| | value |
|---|---|
| regression slope, Δsecurities on Δheadline | **0.021** |
| correlation | **0.132** (Fisher-z **p=0.40**) |
| revisions moving the same direction | **27 of 44** (binomial **p=0.17**) |
| mean \|Δheadline\| | **$229.3bn** |
| mean \|Δdebt securities\| | **$43.5bn** |
| Σ\|Δsecurities\| ÷ Σ\|Δheadline\| | **0.19** |

**A slope of 0.021 means the securities component accounts for two percent of the variation in the
headline revision.** The direction agreement is a coin flip. The magnitude is a fifth. Per edition
the securities share of the year's total restatement runs a median of **0.15**, and the three
editions where it exceeds 0.40 (2023-06-30 at 0.48, 2026-06-30 at 0.41, 2024-06-30 at 1.64) are
the years the *headline* revision was smallest — the securities piece looks large there because
the denominator collapsed, not because the survey did more.

So the honest statement is narrower than the note's own sentence invites: **the June edition
carries the survey, and the survey is not why the June edition is the one that moves.** The
sibling relocated a mechanism it had refuted at its old address; this leg finds it is not the
mechanism at the new one either. What the calendar actually has is a **June revision convention**
whose size is set by the banking and direct-investment forms, with the survey folded in as one
input among several.

**One limit, stated plainly.** This leg tests a *component share*, not a decomposition. Treasury
publishes no revision-source attribution, so a survey contribution could in principle sit inside
the securities line and be offset by unrelated banking moves in the same quarter. What the numbers
rule out is the strong reading — that the survey is what makes the June revision large — not the
weak one, that it contributes something. The measurement that would settle it is a matched diff
of General Government long-term debt securities against the survey's own restatement of Treasury
holdings, which needs the survey's country panel at two vintages and is not attempted here.

### Leg 4 — what the print will actually say · **SUPPORTED**, and a number is registered

The 2027-06-30 release publishes the position **as-of 2027-03-31** (the series runs a one-quarter
lag). The base is the last published first vintage: **$30.196961tn** as-of 2026-03-31, released
2026-06-30, read from `debta2026q1.html`.

Year-over-year growth of the first-vintage headline is the right estimator for a four-quarter
extrapolation, because it nets out the quarterly seasonality the QoQ series carries:

| Window | n | mean YoY | sd |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013Q1–2026Q1 (full) | 53 | +4.87% | 2.17 |
| 2021Q1–2026Q1 | 21 | +6.31% | 2.47 |
| **2023Q1–2026Q1, excluding the 2024Q3 data event and its 2025Q3 mirror** | **11** | **+6.00%** | **0.96** |

The two excluded observations are the same anomaly seen twice: 2024Q3's bad first vintage prints
**+0.58%** YoY, and the following year's comparison against that depressed base prints **+12.91%**.
Neither describes the series. The remaining eleven run **4.27% to 7.47%** with no trend.

```
forecast = $30.197tn × 1.0600 = $32.01tn
±2σ on the clean sample = $31.43tn – $32.59tn
registered band, widened for estimator choice = $31.3tn – $32.7tn
```

**The composition behind it.** As-of 2026Q1 the position splits **General Government $10.584tn**
(of which $8.699tn long-term debt securities), **Central Bank $1.099tn**, **Deposit-taking
corporations $4.458tn**, **Other sectors $12.246tn**, **direct-investment intercompany lending
$1.810tn**, with a market-valued *Debt Securities: By Sector* memorandum of **$14.438tn**. The
series has compounded at 6% through a period in which foreign holdings of long-term U.S.
securities grew 16.7% — external *debt* grows far slower than external *holdings*, because equity
is not debt. That gap is why the annual survey's headline and this one are not the same number
and should never be forecast from one another.

### Leg 5 — does the release move the tape? · **REFUTED**, on the cleanest control this calendar has

The sibling ledger left a standing instruction: *do not test a last-business-day release against
all sessions.* This event permits something better than obeying it. The series publishes **four**
times a year, **all** on a quarter's last business day, and only **one** of the four revises. So
the control is not a synthetic month-end sample — it is **the same release, on the same calendar
position, in the quarters where it does not revise.**

Next-session \|Δ10Y\| on Treasury's daily par curve, releases from 2012-01-01:

| Sample | n | mean \|Δ10Y\| | vs matched control | vs all sessions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **June (revision) editions** | **15** | **5.00bp** | t=+0.27, **p=0.78** | — |
| Other three quarterly editions | 43 | 4.74bp | — | — |
| All quarter-end sessions | 58 | 4.57bp | — | t=+1.48, p=0.14 |
| **All external-debt releases pooled** | 58 | 4.81bp | — | t=+2.04, **p=0.041** |
| All sessions 2012-2026 | 3,671 | 3.89bp | — | — |

Two readings:

1. **The null is clean and the control is exact.** p=0.78. The revision edition and an ordinary
   edition of the same release are the same distribution. The June observations run **2bp to
   12bp** with no year trending.
2. **The sibling's caution reproduces independently, on a different release and a different
   calendar anchor.** Pool all four editions against all sessions and you get **p=0.041** — a
   publishable-looking result that is the quarter-end calendar (p=0.14 on its own, n=58) plus
   small-sample luck, and that vanishes the moment the control is matched. Two ledgers have now
   found this the same way. It is a property of last-business-day releases, not of either release.

**And no tape forward test is registered for this event, deliberately.** The sibling could
register one because its corridor was five tracked events and none was a rate print. This
corridor is **ten**, and three of them own the tape outright: **FOMC minutes publish the same
day** (`fomc-minutes-2027-06-30`, whose own title flags it as the first minutes release in twenty
years to land on a quarter's final session), a `high` **jobs print lands 2027-07-02**, and
**Russell reconstitution** is 2027-06-25. A next-session move on 2027-07-01 would be unattributable
in advance, and registering a test that cannot be scored honestly is worse than not registering
one. The null is stated in the stance and left unregistered, with the reason on the record.

None. `symbols: []`, `low` tier, 16:00 ET publication after the equity cash close, reference data

### Honest limits

- **The 66-for-66 census reads the archive Treasury still serves**, which retains at most three
  vintages per as-of quarter. A revision that was itself superseded before the page was captured
  would be invisible. The claim is therefore "no surviving archived vintage carries a non-June
  stamp," which is what the forward test scores.
- **Six vintage pairs share an identical stamp** (2021Q3, 2021Q4 at 2022-06-30; 2022Q1–Q4 at
  2023-06-30) and are excluded from every statistic above rather than guessed at. They do not
  change the month attribution, because both members of each pair are June.
- **Leg 3 tests a component share, not a decomposition** — see its own limit paragraph.
- **n=11 on the growth estimator** is a small basis for a four-quarter forecast, and the exclusion
  of the 2024Q3 pair is a judgement about what the series is, made explicitly rather than silently.
- **n=15 on the June tape** is the entire published history of the revision edition and is still
  small. The null is stated as a null, not as evidence of equality.

## Stance & kill switches

**Stand aside, permanently, on the trade — and treat this document as the hardening of a sibling's
claim and the death of the mechanism attached to it.** The date is **`confirmed`** (two Treasury
primaries, the last-business-day rule at 55/65 overall and 13/14 on June editions), so the schedule
statement is not hedged; every other statement here is about mechanism, magnitude and measurement,
and none is trading-adjacent in the sense the honesty rules police. Two behavioural instructions
this ledger issues to its successors: **a matched control exists whenever a release recurs on the
same calendar anchor with a known treatment difference — use it instead of a synthetic one**; and
**when a corridor cannot be cleaned, decline to register the test and say why**, rather than
registering one whose kill can never be attributed.

**Kill switches:**

1. **The date.** No gross-external-debt release published on **2027-06-30** — a Washington D.C.
   closure, a funding lapse, or a schedule revision. Kills leg 1 and makes FT-…-3 and FT-…-4
   unscoreable.
2. **The June monopoly.** Any of the **2026-09-30**, **2026-12-31** or **2027-03-31** releases
   publishing a revised vintage for a previously published as-of quarter. That breaks a 66-for-66
   record at its first live test and moves leg 2 from "census" back to "convention."
3. **The mechanism refutation.** The 2027-06-30 edition's four revised quarters lifting the pooled
   regression slope of Δdebt-securities on Δheadline above **0.30**, or the correlation to
   **p < 0.05**. That would say the survey's contribution has become the driver, and the fifteen
   years behind it were the anomaly.
4. **The level forecast.** A first-vintage headline for as-of 2027-03-31 outside
   **$31.3tn–$32.7tn**. Below is the post-2023 growth regime breaking; above is another data event
   of the 2024Q3 kind, which the band's width already anticipates but does not cover in full.
5. **The December hypothesis.** A **June** edition slipping more than **two days** off its
   scheduled last business day, which would say the slip risk is not concentrated in December and
   that leg 1's promotion rested on a coincidence rather than a structural difference.
