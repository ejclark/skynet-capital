## Initial research

### The question

Two sibling sweeps proposed this event on the same day.
[`tic-quarterly-external-debt-2026-09-30`](tic-quarterly-external-debt-2026-09-30.md) filed it because
it found this edition **was not in the calendar at all** despite carrying the series' entire schedule
risk. [`tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-03-31`](tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-03-31.md) filed it as
**a hole**: the 2027-06-30 ledger's registered test names three intermediate checkpoints and only two
had entries. Both proposals also flagged, and neither resolved, a contradiction they had both noticed:
Treasury's statistics page says December releases at **10 AM** while the release-dates page says all
releases occur at **4 p.m.**

So the question this session owns is the one both proposals left open. **Is the December edition
genuinely a different animal — in when it prints, in how reliably it prints, and in what the number is
worth — or is it three siblings' framing of a fourth interchangeable member?**

### One-line verdict

It is genuinely different on all three, and the difference has one shape: **December is where this
series keeps its entire tail** — the only edition that prints inside the session, the only one that has
ever been materially late, and the publisher of the quarter with the smallest typical revision and the
largest ever.

### Method

Sourced web research per [`EVENT-RESEARCH.md`](../../process/EVENT-RESEARCH.md) (macro-print mode — no
symbol instruments exist for this kind; `symbols: []`). Primaries fetched **direct** this session
(2026-09-09, plain curl, HTTP 200): Treasury's TIC release-dates table (**111,181 bytes**) with its
full REVISIONS-and-RELEASE-DATES note block, and Treasury's *U.S. Gross External Debt* statistics page
(**174,127 bytes**). **All 134** archived Table A vintages named by that page's index were re-fetched

daily par-yield-curve CSVs for **2011–2026** (3,922 sessions) and CBOE's `VIX_History.csv`. Every

Three method points differ from the siblings and each one changed an answer:

1. **The release-dates table was parsed by cell index, not as prose.** Its six columns are Month ·
   Monthly · Quarterly-nonfinancial · **Gross external debt** · Annual-prelim · Annual-final. That is
   how the December 2026 row's `31` is placed in the right column with certainty — and how the January
   2026 row's `16 (delayed)` is shown to sit in the **gross-external-debt column specifically**, which
   is the primary-sourced proof that the +16 slip is Treasury's own flagged delay of *this* release
   rather than a monthly one.
2. **Federal holidays were constructed from 5 U.S.C. 6103 including the observed-holiday rule in both
   directions**, notably that a **Saturday January 1 makes the preceding December 31 an observed
   holiday**. That single rule is what corrects a sibling's December census (leg 3).
3. **The headline total is read from a label whitelist with a magnitude guard.** The BPM5-era files
   label it `GROSS EXTERNAL DEBT *`, the BPM6 files `Gross External Debt Position`, and two files wrap
   the label across two lines; the page *header* also contains the string, followed by a year. Without
   a guard rejecting any candidate under $1tn, `debta2013q2` silently returns **2013** as a level. All
   **134 of 134** vintages parse with it, against the siblings' 97 and 132.

### Leg 2 — this is the only edition that prints inside the session · **SUPPORTED**, and it invalidates a ground three siblings used

Treasury's *U.S. Gross External Debt* statistics page, fetched direct this session, says it in one
sentence:

> *"These position data are reported as-of the end of each quarter, with a one-quarter lag. Releases
> are made at 4 PM in Washington D.C. on the last business day of March, June, and September, and at
> **10 AM** on the last business day of December."*

The release-dates page's blanket header — *"All data releases occur at 4 p.m. Washington, D.C.
time"* — is the generic statement for the whole TIC family; the statistics page is the
release-specific one and it names this edition explicitly. Where a generic and a specific primary
disagree, the specific one governs. Both proposals noticed the contradiction; neither resolved it, and
[`tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-03-31`](tic-quarterly-external-debt-2027-03-31.md) quoted the same
sentence while reading only the half that put *its* edition in the 4 PM group.

**Why this is not a trivia point.** All three sibling ledgers list *"16:00 ET publication after the
equity cash close"* among the grounds for standing aside, and all three run their tape test on the
**next** session, because a 4 PM release cannot move the session it lands in. For this edition both
moves are wrong. A 10:00 ET publication lands roughly 90 minutes into the cash session and about four
hours before the SIFMA-recommended early bond close on 12-31 — so if this release has a tape
signature at all, it is on the **release day itself**, in a window no sibling has measured. Leg 6 runs
it.

The stand-aside survives, and it survives on better evidence than the ground it loses. But the ground
it loses was load-bearing on three documents, which is the finding.

### Leg 3 — December's fragility, its mechanism, and a sibling census corrected · **SUPPORTED**

**First, the correction.** [`tic-quarterly-external-debt-2026-09-30`](tic-quarterly-external-debt-2026-09-30.md)
reports December's exceptions as `−1, −1, +16, +31`, naming **2021-12-30** and **2024-12-30** as early
publications. Recomputing the last business day from statute leaves only one. **2022-01-01 was a
Saturday**, so under 5 U.S.C. 6103 the preceding **Friday 2021-12-31 was the observed New Year's Day
holiday** — making Thursday 2021-12-30 the last business day of December 2021 and that publication
**+0**, exactly on schedule. The 2027-03-31 sibling, which built its holidays the same way, lists three
December exceptions rather than four; this session reproduces that.

**Treasury's own schedule is the proof.** Its 2027 table reads `December | 16 | 16 | 30` — the gross
external debt date is the **30th**, not the 31st, and 2027-12-31 is a Friday. The only reason a Friday
is not a business day is that **2028-01-01 falls on a Saturday**, making 2027-12-31 the observed
holiday. Treasury applies the rule this session applied; the sibling's `−1` was an artefact of a
holiday set, not an early publication.

**Second, the fragility itself is real and it is entirely December's.** The two remaining exceptions
are the only publications in fifteen years more than two days off schedule:

| Scheduled | Published | Offset | Funding lapse inside the reference-close → release window |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013-12-31 | 2013-12-31 | **+0** | 2013-10-01 → 2013-10-17, **16 days** |
| 2018-12-31 | **2019-01-31** | **+31** | 2018-12-22 → 2019-01-25, **35 days** — the release date sat **inside** the lapse |
| 2025-12-31 | **2026-01-16** | **+16** | 2025-10-01 → 2025-11-12, **43 days** — the lapse ended **49 days before** the scheduled date |
| the other 12 | on schedule | +0 | none |

The +16 is not this session's inference: parsing the release-dates table by cell index puts
`16 (delayed)` in the **gross-external-debt column** of the January 2026 row, which is Treasury
flagging its own delay of this exact release. Lapse dates are the repo's own, from
[`government-funding-deadline-2026-09-30`](government-funding-deadline-2026-09-30.md) (which records
the 2025 lapse at ~43 days, 2025-10-01 → 2025-11-12) and
[`cr-expiry-2026-12-11`](cr-expiry-2026-12-11.md); they are `NEWS:`-grade, not Treasury primaries, and
are labelled as such.

**Third — and this is what neither proposal had — the mechanism is structural and specific to this
edition.** Both proposals wrote that the two slips are "appropriations events" and stopped. The two are
not the same event: one release fell *inside* a lapse, the other fell **seven weeks after one ended**.
What they share is the window:

> **The December edition is the only one of the four whose reference-quarter-close to release window
> contains October 1** — the federal fiscal-year boundary, and the one date on which a US funding lapse
> can begin by statute. Q3 closes 09-30 and the release is 12-31, so 10-01 is always inside. March's
> window (12-31 → 03-31), June's (03-31 → 06-30) and September's (06-30 → 09-30) contain no
> fiscal-year boundary at all.

That explains why the fragility is December's and nowhere else's, and it covers both slips — the 2019
one directly, the 2026 one through the compilation backlog a 43-day lapse leaves behind. It also
explains why the effect is not universal: **2013's lapse was in the same window and produced +0.** So
the honest claim is duration-conditioned, and its arithmetic is small enough to state in full:
**lapses of 35 and 43 days → slip (2 of 2); a lapse of 16 days → no slip (0 of 1); n=3.** That is a
conditioned regularity on three observations, registered as such in **FT-…-2** and not dressed as
more.

**It is live for this print.** [`cr-expiry-2026-12-11`](cr-expiry-2026-12-11.md) is `estimate`, `high`
impact, and its own research establishes that funding runs *through* Friday 2026-12-11, so a lapse
would begin **00:00 on 2026-12-12** and this release would fall on its **day 20**. The 2018
configuration is the structural match — a lapse beginning in December with the release date inside
it — and it is the one that produced **+31**. Nothing here forecasts whether the lapse happens: that
event is `estimate`, its own ledger declines to inherit a base case, and an estimate widens caution
without licensing anything.

### Leg 5 — what the print will actually say · **SUPPORTED**, and a number is registered

### Leg 6 — does the release move the tape? · **REFUTED**, and the significant-looking version of the test is the one to discard

Leg 2 established that this is the only edition published inside the session, so the treatment window
is the **release day itself**. Both windows were run, on Treasury's daily par curve, for the 57
publications from 2012-01-01:

| Window | December editions | Other three editions | All sessions | Dec vs editions | Dec vs all sessions |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Same session (the 10 AM window)** | **4.21bp** (n=14) | **2.90bp** (n=39) | 3.89bp (n=3,672) | t=+1.98, **p=0.048** | t=+0.59, **p=0.552** |
| Next session (what the siblings measured) | 4.43bp (n=14) | 4.62bp (n=39) | 3.89bp | t=−0.20, p=0.839 | t=+0.67, p=0.503 |

**The same-session matched control produces a nominally significant result, and it should be thrown
away.** The reason is visible in the table: the control group's mean is **2.90bp against an ordinary
session's 3.89bp**, which is itself significant at **p=0.013**. The Mar/Jun/Sep sessions are not a
neutral baseline — they are unusually *quiet* quarter-ends, and they are quiet in a window that
contains **no treatment at all**, because those editions publish after the close. Comparing a treated
group against an anomalous untreated one manufactures a difference out of the control's oddity, not
the treatment's effect.

Against the population, December's own sessions are ordinary: **4.21bp vs 3.89bp, p=0.552**. And the
distribution is the plainest statement available for a stand-aside — the fourteen observations run
**0, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 7, 7, 7 bp**. **In fourteen years, the session carrying this
release has never moved the 10-year more than 7bp.**

**The standing caution this generalises.** The 2027-03-31 sibling's instruction was *split a pooled
statistic before citing it*. This is the next case of the same family and it needs its own sentence:
**a matched control is only worth having if the control group is itself ordinary.** Check the control
against the population before trusting the comparison — otherwise a quiet control and a normal
treatment produce a finding out of nothing. Three ledgers have now examined last-business-day releases
and none has found an effect that survives two controls.

**No tape forward test is registered, deliberately** — the same call all three siblings made. The
corridor is **24** tracked events over ±5 days and several own the tape outright: `medium` **FOMC
minutes** the day before (`fomc-minutes-2026-12-30`), a `high` **ISM Manufacturing** five days after,
three coupon auctions in the preceding week, and a year-end session carrying **Russell style capping**,
the **S&P select-sector secondary reweight**, a **SIFMA-recommended early bond close** and tax-loss
flow. A same-session move on 2026-12-31 would be unattributable in advance, and the early close
truncates the very window a 10:00 ET release would act in.

### Honest limits

- **The fragility claim rests on n=3.** Three December windows in the record contain a funding lapse;
  two slipped and one did not. The 35/43-vs-16-day split is the honest reading of three observations,
  not a threshold anyone has demonstrated. A fourth observation could move it either way, and
  **FT-…-2** is written to be scoreable in both directions.
- **The 2026-01-16 mechanism is inferred, not sourced.** Treasury flags the delay and gives no reason.
  That a 43-day lapse ending seven weeks earlier caused it through a compilation backlog is the most
  parsimonious story available, and it is a story. The alternative — an unrelated operational
  delay — is not excluded by anything here.
- **Lapse dates are `NEWS:`-grade**, taken from this repo's own funding ledgers rather than from a
  Treasury or OMB primary fetched this session.
- **The as-of-Q3 skew is a description of a distribution containing one outlier.** Drop 2024Q3 and Q3
  looks like Q4. That is exactly the claim — the tail *is* the finding — but it means the result is
  one observation deep and should never be quoted as a mean effect.
- **The same-session test cannot separate a release effect from an intraday quarter-end effect**, only
  from the population and from the other editions. Its power is low: n=14 against a 1.97bp sd detects
  nothing under about 2bp.
- **The 10 AM publication time is one primary's sentence.** No timestamped observation of an actual
  December posting was obtained, so the *time* is Treasury's claim, corroborated by nothing else.
- **The level band's coverage is not stated as a historical frequency.** ±2σ on n=10 is a
  normal-theory interval on a series with a documented fat tail (2024Q3), and the registered band does
  not pretend to cover a repeat of it.
- **Two of the three level estimators share a sample** with the sibling ledgers. The interpolation
  check tests coherence between documents, not correctness of any of them.
