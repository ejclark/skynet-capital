## At a glance

**TL;DR.** **Read it, do not trade it — but of the four 2027 editions, this is the one to
calendar.** SLOOS's special-question calendar turns out to be **seasonal**, and that is this
session's finding: the January edition carries a **year-ahead outlook block** — bank expectations
for lending standards, demand and asset quality over the coming year — in **17 of 17** editions
from 2010 to 2026, with no exception; the July edition carries a standing levels block the Fed
itself introduces with *"As with all July surveys"*; April and October are ad hoc, and **4 of 9**
April editions ran no special questions at all. Every other SLOOS question looks backward at the
past quarter, so **the winter edition is the only forward-looking SLOOS of the year.** On the tape
it is still nothing: winter-edition KRE excess vs SPY is **−0.336%, p=0.350** against non-SLOOS
Mondays, and every other instrument is flat. The one result that looked real is the cautionary
tale — winter ^VIX runs **+7.095%, p=0.012** and it *survives* the weekday-matched control the
[May sibling](sloos-2027-05-03.md) introduced; drop **one** date, **2018-02-05** (the largest
one-day VIX move in the whole 2010–2026 window at **+115.60%**, the XIV/"VIXmageddon" session, a
SLOOS Monday by coincidence), and it is **+0.314%, p=0.486**. Leave-one-out finds no other date
that matters. **The rule this buys the calendar: weekday-matching is necessary but not
sufficient — on a subset of n ≤ 20, run leave-one-out before believing anything.** Date is an
**`estimate`** and stays one.

## Initial research

### The question, plainly

SLOOS is expected at 14:00 ET on **2027-02-01**, the Monday after the January 26–27 FOMC. It
reached this calendar hours ago: the [`sloos-2027-05-03`](sloos-2027-05-03.md) initial research
proposed it on **2026-09-09** as *"the leading indicator for sloos-2027-05-03"* and *"the earliest
place to see whether the NONDEPOSITORY FINANCIAL INSTITUTION special questions… have become a
standing series."*

That sibling did the heavy general work: it audited the release-date rule against 67 releases,
measured the release-day null on the same 67, and caught the Monday-effect trap that had made
SLOOS look like a volatility event. So the general questions are answered and re-deriving them
would be waste. The questions left are **subset** questions: **is this edition's date right on its
own class; is this edition different from the other three in content; is it different on the tape;
and is the reason the proposal gave for its existence actually the right one?**

**One-line verdict:** the date is right and the winter subset audits 9-for-9 with a day-for-day
2021 calendar twin as corroboration; the edition **is** structurally different — it is the only
forward-looking SLOOS of the year, 17-for-17 since 2010 — it is **not** different on the tape, and
the reason the proposal gave for it is the one thing here that is wrong, because January's
special-question slot is never free.

### Method

Sourced primary research per [`EVENT-RESEARCH.md`](../../process/EVENT-RESEARCH.md) (macro-print
mode), plus two first-party measurements. Every federalreserve.gov and home.treasury.gov fetch
returned HTTP 200 to plain `curl` on **2026-09-09**; nothing was blocked.

1. **Edition-page corpus.** All **122** SLOOS edition pages linked from
   `federalreserve.gov/data/sloos.htm` — `/data/sloos/sloos-YYYYMM.htm` for the modern series and
   `/boarddocs/SnLoanSurvey/YYYYMM/default.htm` back to 1997 — fetched (122 of 122 HTTP 200) and
   parsed for three things: the `Last update` field (the release date), the *"respondent banks
   received the survey on X… responses were due by Y"* footnote, and **every special-question
   header and its surrounding prose**. That third extraction is what the sibling's study did not
   do and is where this session's finding comes from.
2. **Winter-subset release-day study.** Yahoo dividend-adjusted daily closes via
   `scripts/research/market-data.mjs` → `bars()`, instrument caches busted first per the
   cache-discipline rule. Instruments **KRE · KBE · XLF · SPY · QQQ · ^VIX · TLT**, window
   2010-01-01 → 2026-09-09 (4,197 sessions). Event set: the **17** January editions released
   2010-02-01 → 2026-02-02, tested against three pools — all sessions, all Mondays, and Mondays
   that are not any SLOOS release — by two-sided permutation test, 200,000 iterations, plus a
   **leave-one-out jackknife** over all 17 dates.
3. **Calendars.** `monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm` (HTTP 200, 164,831 bytes) for the 2026 and
   2027 FOMC dates; `newsevents/2027-january.htm` and `newsevents/2027-february.htm` (both HTTP
   404) for the absent 2027 release calendar;
   `home.treasury.gov/…/most-recent-quarterly-refunding-documents` (HTTP 200, 73,662 bytes) for the
   refunding pairing behind the adjacency proposed here.

### Conviction legs, tested

**1. The date is right, and the winter subset audits 9-for-9 with a calendar twin — SUPPORTED.**
The Fed's published 2027 FOMC calendar reads *"January 26-27"*, and the release rule the sibling
repaired (the Monday five sessions after the FOMC's final day, shifted to the next business day
only when that Monday is a federal holiday or the market is shut) gives Monday **2027-02-01**,
which is not a federal holiday. Re-audited here on this event's own class rather than on the full
set — the nine January editions whose release dates the modern pages carry:

| Edition | FOMC ended | Derived Monday | Actual release |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan-2018 | 2018-01-31 | 2018-02-05 | **2018-02-05** |
| Jan-2019 | 2019-01-30 | 2019-02-04 | **2019-02-04** |
| Jan-2020 | 2020-01-29 | 2020-02-03 | **2020-02-03** |
| Jan-2021 | 2021-01-27 | 2021-02-01 | **2021-02-01** |
| Jan-2022 | 2022-01-26 | 2022-01-31 | **2022-01-31** |
| Jan-2023 | 2023-02-01 | 2023-02-06 | **2023-02-06** |
| Jan-2024 | 2024-01-31 | 2024-02-05 | **2024-02-05** |
| Jan-2025 | 2025-01-29 | 2025-02-03 | **2025-02-03** |
| Jan-2026 | 2026-01-28 | 2026-02-02 | **2026-02-02** |

Nine for nine, no exceptions — and none of the sibling's three business-day-shift misses is a
winter edition, because no US federal holiday falls in the 2027-02-01 window. All **17** January
editions 2010→2026 released on a **Monday**, without exception.

The corroboration worth more than the arithmetic: **January 2027 is a day-for-day repeat of
January 2021.** Both are non-leap years beginning on a Friday, so every date carries the same
weekday; both have a January FOMC on the **26th–27th**; and the January 2021 edition released
**2021-02-01**. The nearest calendar analogue that actually happened produced exactly this date.

**The entry stays `estimate`.** `newsevents/2027-january.htm` and `newsevents/2027-february.htm`
both returned **HTTP 404** on 2026-09-09, so no primary carries the date or the 14:00 ET slot; the
FOMC calendar it derives from says *"each meeting date is tentative until confirmed at the meeting
immediately preceding it"*; and this lane does not self-confirm what it derived. The canonical
`sloos-2027-02-01.json` is written in this PR from the one proposal that named it, which becomes
inert.

**2. SLOOS's special-question calendar is SEASONAL, and the winter edition is the only
forward-looking one — SUPPORTED, and this is the session's finding.** Extracting every
special-question header from all 122 edition pages produces a pattern neither sibling ledger had
looked for. Two of the four quarterly slots are **standing**:

| Slot | Standing block | Record |
|---|---|---|
| **January** | *"Special Questions on Banks' Outlook for `<year>`"* — expectations for lending standards, borrower demand and **asset quality** over the coming year, *"assuming that economic activity evolves in line with consensus forecasts"* | **17 of 17**, 2010→2026. Under that exact header 2016→2026; as a differently-worded year-ahead block in 2010 (*"Outlook for Asset Quality"*), 2011 (*"outlook for credit quality in 2011"*), 2012, 2013 (*"outlook for asset quality… during 2013"*), 2014 (*"outlook for loan performance… over 2014"*) and 2015 (*"expectations for loan delinquency and charge-off rates in 2015"*) |
| **July** | *"Special Questions on Current Level of Banks' Lending Standards"* — where standards sit against their own historical range | **9 of 9**, 2018→2026. The Fed introduces it in the July-2026 edition with *"As with all July surveys"* |
| April | ad hoc | **4 of 9 carried no special questions at all** (2020, 2022, 2024, 2025) |
| October | ad hoc | credit-card/auto policies is the only soft recurrence (2018, 2019, 2022, 2023) |

Every other question SLOOS asks is retrospective — what standards and demand did over the past
three months. The January block is the only place the survey asks banks what they **expect**. That
makes the winter edition structurally different from its three siblings in a way that has nothing
to do with the tape, and it is the honest answer to "why calendar this one."

The July slot deserves its own note because it changes what a sibling ledger concluded, and the
change is a refinement rather than a contradiction (**see leg 6**).

**4. The one result that looked real is a single day — SUPPORTED as a refutation, and it is the
transferable half of this session.** Run the same study on ^VIX and the winter subset does
something the full set does not:

| Pool | Winter ^VIX mean | p | n |
|---|---|---|---|
| vs **all sessions** (+0.307%) | **+7.095%** | **0.004** | 17 |
| vs the **Monday** pool (+1.878%) | **+7.095%** | **0.034** | 17 |
| vs **non-SLOOS Mondays** (+1.756%) | **+7.095%** | **0.012** | 17 |

That last row is the tell that this is not the sibling's trap repeating: the other 46 editions sit
*exactly* on the Monday baseline (p=0.986), so weekday-matching has already done its work and
something appears to be left over on the winter subset alone. It is one date:

| Winter subset, ^VIX | mean | p (vs non-SLOOS Mondays) |
|---|---|---|
| all 17 | +7.095% | **0.012** |
| **ex 2018-02-05** (n=16) | **+0.314%** | **0.486** |
| ex 2018-02-05 and 2014-02-03 (n=15) | −0.763% | 0.242 |

**2018-02-05 is the single largest one-day VIX move in the entire 2010→2026 window — +115.60%**,
verified first-party against all 4,197 sessions in the pull (the next four are 2024-12-18 +74.04%,
2024-08-05 +64.90%, 2021-01-27 +61.64%, 2021-11-26 +54.04%). It is the XIV/"VIXmageddon" session,
and it is a winter-SLOOS Monday by coincidence. A leave-one-out jackknife across all 17 dates
finds **no other** date whose removal takes the result above p=0.05.

**The rule this buys the calendar, and it is the next rung on the sibling's own:** weekday-matching
is **necessary but not sufficient**. The sibling's leg 3 established that a fixed-weekday event
must be tested against its weekday's pool; this session shows that a *subset* small enough to be
interesting — n≈17, which is what you get whenever you slice a quarterly release by season — has
no defence against a single p99.9 session, and will hand you p=0.012 anyway. **After
weekday-matching, run leave-one-out before believing a subset.** The cost is one loop; the thing it
prevents is a ledger claiming that winter SLOOS releases are volatility events.

**5. The collection window closes about 2027-01-04 — the outlook block is answered over the
holidays and before the meeting it is timed for — SUPPORTED.** Six January editions publish their
own sent and due dates:

| Edition | Sent | Due | Released | Due-lag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan-2021 | 2020-12-14 | 2021-01-04 | 2021-02-01 | 28 d |
| Jan-2022 | 2021-12-13 | 2021-12-30 | 2022-01-31 | 32 d |
| Jan-2023 | 2022-12-19 | 2023-01-06 | 2023-02-06 | 31 d |
| Jan-2024 | 2023-12-18 | 2024-01-09 | 2024-02-05 | 27 d |
| Jan-2025 | 2024-12-16 | 2025-01-03 | 2025-02-03 | 31 d |
| Jan-2026 | 2025-12-10 | 2026-01-02 | 2026-02-02 | 31 d |

The questionnaire goes out **mid-December every year, straddling the December FOMC** (sometimes the
day before it, sometimes five days after), and responses close in the **first days of January**.
Applied to a 2027-02-01 release with a **2026-12-08/09** December FOMC: **sent ≈ mid-December 2026,
responses due ≈ 2027-01-04** — which is, exactly, the date the day-for-day twin Jan-2021 edition
published.

Two consequences, and the second is the one that matters. First, the *staleness* is structural
rather than winter-specific: every SLOOS closes ~31 days before release and therefore ~26 days
before the meeting whose Monday it follows, so "it is a month stale" is a fact about the survey,
not about this edition. Second, and specific to this edition: the **"Outlook for 2027" answers are
written with the December 2026 decision in hand and roughly 23 days before the 2027-01-27
meeting** — so the year-ahead expectations are conditioned on December, on the Fed's published
consensus-forecast assumption, and on nothing that happens in January. That is what the block is
worth reading as, and it is why the release landing five sessions after a decision does not make it
a read on that decision.

**6. A refinement to the sibling's NDFI claim, and it undercuts the reason this event was proposed
— MIXED, and it is reported, not acted on.** [`sloos-2027-05-03`](sloos-2027-05-03.md) leg 6 found
that the **April-2026** and **July-2026** editions both carried nondepository-financial-institution
special questions, called two consecutive editions a series, and registered recurrence as
**FT-sloos-2027-05-03-3** — whose kill switch scores on whether the **2026-11-02**, **2027-02-01**
or **2027-05-03** editions carry NDFI questions. The proposal that created *this* event named it
*"the earliest place to see whether the NDFI special questions… have become a standing series."*

Both halves of the underlying observation reproduce here. What the header extraction adds is that
the two editions are not the same kind of thing:

- **April 2026** ran a standalone block, *"Special Questions on Lending to Nondepository Financial
  Institutions"* — a genuinely new set.
- **July 2026**'s NDFI content (9 uses of the abbreviation) sits **inside** *"Special Questions on
  Current Level of Banks' Lending Standards"*, the standing July block, which that edition
  introduces with *"As with all July surveys."* NDFIs are a **category added to a recurring
  levels survey**, not a second standalone set.

So "two consecutive editions is a series" mixes one new instrument with one standing instrument
extended to a new category. That does not kill the sibling's hypothesis — the underlying question,
*is the Fed now systematically surveying bank credit to private-credit intermediaries*, is still
live and still the most interesting thing about the document. It changes **where you can test it**,
and the answer is not here: **January's special-question slot is occupied 17 times out of 17 by the
year-ahead outlook block**, so an absence of NDFI questions on 2027-02-01 is weak evidence about a
series and strong evidence only about a crowded questionnaire. The **October** edition
(**2026-11-02**) has a genuinely ad-hoc slot and is the fair test of the three.

This is written down and nothing else. Rows are append-only, its forward test is its lane's file
and its lane's call, and one file per owner — the number belongs in this ledger, the amendment
belongs to whoever next pulses [sloos-2026-11-02](sloos-2026-11-02.md) or the May sibling.

**7. The January-2026 AI questions are the first SLOOS content nominally about this book's own
names, and they are still not tradable — MIXED.** The January 2026 edition carried a **second**
special-question set alongside the outlook block, on *"banks' likelihood of approving loans to
firms with various levels of exposure to artificial intelligence (AI) in comparison to the
beginning of 2025 and their assessment of the impact of AI on selected industries."* Banks
reported being, on net, **more likely to approve** C&I loans to firms benefiting from high AI
exposure, **less likely** for firms adversely affected, unchanged for firms with little exposure,
and reported AI having a beneficial effect for **every** queried sector.

Every one of the ten symbols in this book's earnings calendar — NVDA, MRVL, AVGO, MSFT, GOOG,
META, AMZN, AAPL, CRWV, MU — is an AI-exposure name, so this is the closest SLOOS has come to
asking a question about the book. The honest discount is large: nine of the ten fund themselves in
public capital markets rather than the surveyed bank channel, the tenth (**CRWV**) funds through
private credit and asset-backed markets, the question is qualitative and quarterly, and leg 3 says
the release day is an ordinary session. **It raises the reading priority of the winter edition and
nothing else.** Whether it recurs is registered as a forward test rather than assumed — one
appearance is one appearance, and the nine-year gap between the 2016 and 2026 NDFI questions is
this survey's own base rate for a topic of the moment.

**treasury-7y-note-2027-01-28**, **japan-cpi-tokyo-flash-2027-01-29**,

The session class carries its own drift with no release attached: first-trading-day-of-month

it. The point is that **the session class routinely produces large moves that a SLOOS ledger would
be wrong to book**, and 2027-02-01 is that session class with three releases on it.

### What plays the conditions support (date is an `estimate`)

None directional. No house playbook is macro-keyed (S1/S2/E1/S3/S4 + G1 are symbol- and
earnings-keyed). A reading assignment and two disciplines:

- **Calendar the 14:00 ET release on 2027-02-01 as the year's SLOOS reading date**, ahead of the
  April and July editions — it is the only one that publishes bank *expectations*. Extract the
  year-ahead standards/demand/asset-quality lines, and check whether the AI question set recurs.
- **After weekday-matching, jackknife.** Leg 4's finding, and it is the more valuable of the two
  because it is not about SLOOS: every subset study on this calendar — by season, by month-turn, by
  regime — lands in the n≈15–25 range where one p99 session decides the p-value.
- **A crowded questionnaire is a confound for a content forward test.** Leg 6's finding: testing
  "does topic X recur" on an edition whose special-question slot is 17-for-17 committed measures
  the questionnaire's capacity, not the Fed's interest.

Explicitly **not** claimed: that the winter edition is more market-moving than the others. Leg 3
says it is not, and leg 4 says the one measurement suggesting otherwise is a single 2018 session.
"The most informative edition" and "the tradable edition" are different claims and only the first
is made here.

### Honest limits

- **The winter subset is n=17 and this session went looking for a subset effect.** Slicing a
  quarterly release four ways and testing six instruments against three pools is ~72 tests; a
  p=0.012 in that space is not a finding, which is exactly why leg 4 spends its length killing its
  own result rather than reporting it.
- **Daily bars cannot see a 14:00 ET release.** T+0 close-to-close contains six hours of unrelated
  trading before the survey publishes and two after it — and on this date, an ISM manufacturing
  print at 10:00 ET inside that window. The measurement supports *"no large release-day effect,"*
  never *"no effect."* Intraday (14:00→16:00) data would settle it and was not used.
- **Release dates are `Last update` fields, not a published release calendar.** The sibling
  cross-validated them 13-for-13 against the Fed's own newsevents calendars for 2023-05 → 2026-08;
  that validation is inherited here, not re-run, and it does not cover the pre-2023 winter
  editions. The April-2018 page's `Last update` is a June-2019 revision and its release date is
  rule-derived; it is not a winter edition and does not enter this session's n=17.
