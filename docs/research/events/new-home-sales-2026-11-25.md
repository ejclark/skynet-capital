## Initial research

### The question, plainly

This event exists because the `construction-spending-2026-12-01` lane's adjacency sweep proposed it,
on a compositional argument: the residential leg is the largest single component of construction
put-in-place — $871.2B of $2,157.6B SAAR, **40.4%** — and it is the leg falling fastest (−7.3% y/y)
while office and power sit at all-time highs. This calendar tracked the resale side of housing
demand and the residential supply side, and had **no new-home demand entry at all**. The sweep's
words: this print "reports the same October 2026 reference month, so the two read the same month
from the demand and the put-in-place side."

**Is the release real and regular, does it reach the nowcast, does it reach a price — and is the
number it publishes worth reading on the day it lands?**

**One-line verdict:** it is real, regular and separable on both the model and the tape, which no
other print in this family has managed — and the two answers it gives are "yes, on the residential
contribution only" and a well-powered "no" on the price, on a statistic Census itself declines to
call different from zero 37 times in 40.

### Leg 3 — the footprint is real, lands on residential, and *reduces* the headline move · **SUPPORTED, with a twist**

`ContribArchives` gives 1,822 same-quarter deltas. Classifying each vintage by its own `Data
releases` free text (the release is spelled 21 different ways across the decade — "New homes",
"New-home sales/prices/costs", "New home sales/construct. costs" — all matched):

| Vintage class | n | \|Δ Residential\| | \|Δ Fixed inv\| | \|Δ GDP\| |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **New-home sales named ALONE** | **93** | **0.0543pp** | 0.0559pp | **0.0528pp** |
| New-home sales named at all | 138 | 0.0507pp | 0.0544pp | 0.0692pp |
| Every vintage naming no new-home sales | 1,684 | **0.0058pp** | 0.0286pp | **0.0961pp** |

| Comparison (20,000-iteration permutation on medians) | \|Δ Resid\| | \|Δ Fixed\| | \|Δ GDP\| |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alone vs. vintages naming no new-home sales | **p<0.0001** | **p=0.0026** | **p=0.0287** |
| Named-at-all vs. vintages naming no new-home sales | **p<0.0001** | **p=0.0020** | p=0.0274 |

Three readings, in descending order of usefulness.

**The residential effect is unambiguous.** A 9.4× ratio on medians with p<0.0001, and the shape of
the distribution agrees: **57.7%** of baseline vintages move residential by under 0.01pp against
**10.9%** of new-home-sales vintages, and **78.3%** of new-home-sales vintages clear 0.02pp. The
model reads this print, and it reads it on exactly the line the release is about.

**The headline result is stronger than a null — it points the other way.** The isolated class moves
\|Δ GDP\| **0.0528pp** against a **0.0961pp** baseline, **p=0.0287**. This is not
"cannot reject zero difference" (construction spending's p=0.77); it is a *significantly quieter*
headline day. The mechanism is not mysterious: a new-home-sales-alone vintage is a day on which the
model updated one small investment line and nothing else, whereas the baseline is dominated by
trigger days carrying employment, CPI, trade and retail.

**And on 2026-11-25's own shape, the residential move is this print's and the headline move is not.**
The date bundles new-home sales with **personal income and outlays**, a trigger. Historically that
class is n=13:

| Class | n | \|Δ Residential\| | \|Δ GDP\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| **New-home sales + personal income/outlays** | **13** | **0.0820pp** | **0.4340pp** |
| Personal income/outlays **without** new-home sales | 84 | **0.0018pp** | **0.3404pp** |
| Baseline (no new-home sales) | 1,684 | 0.0058pp | 0.0961pp |

Residential: **p=0.0003** — adding this print to a personal-income day moves residential 45× harder.
GDP: **p=0.5973** — adding this print changes the headline move not at all. If the 11-25 GDPNow
vintage swings, that is Personal Income's doing, and a reader who attributes it to new-home sales
will have learned the wrong lesson.

One observation is recorded rather than leaned on. **All 11 November-dated new-home-sales vintages**
in the archive exist, and their median \|Δ residential\| is **0.1343pp** against **0.0461pp** for
non-November ones (**p=0.0004**). Unlike the sibling's December run, this one *survives* two
controls: restricted to vintages **not** bundled with personal income / NIPA / a GDP estimate,
November still reads **0.1083pp (n=7) vs 0.0442pp (n=118), p=0.0112**; and the **baseline** is not
itself elevated in November (0.0072pp vs 0.0058pp, **p=0.4537**), so it is not a calendar artifact.
What stops it becoming a call is that no mechanism was identified and the effect is concentrated in
recent years — the 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2024 Novembers each rank **1st or 2nd loudest in their own
year**, while 2015, 2018 and 2019 ranked in the bottom half, and the whole 2021+ era is louder
(0.0640pp vs 0.0388pp, p=0.0310). `-1` is therefore registered at the pooled 0.02pp bar, not at the
November median.

That is a better outcome than the sibling's declined claim: a measured null across eight instruments
is a fact you can stand on, where an unmeasurable claim is only an absence.

### Honest limits

- **The tape null is a null.** Eight instruments failing to reject at n=65 is a strong absence of
  evidence, but the smallest effect this design could detect is not computed here, and a real
  intraday effect confined to the 10:00–10:15 window would be invisible in a daily bar. The claim is
  "the session does not widen," not "no price moves at 10:00."
- **The identification on the nowcast is far weaker than the sibling's.** Only **23.9%** of
  new-home-sales vintages clear the baseline's residential p90, against **91.1%** for construction
  spending on structures. The median effect is large and the distribution is clearly shifted, but
  you could not read the release calendar off the residential column.
- **The November result has two surviving controls and no mechanism.** It beats the co-release
  control (n=7 unbundled, p=0.0112) and the baseline-November control (p=0.4537), which is more than
  the sibling's December run managed — but the effect is concentrated post-2021, the unbundled cell
  is seven rows, and month-2-of-quarter vintages as a class are elevated (0.0843pp, n=46) on rows
  that substantially overlap. Registered at the pooled bar for exactly that reason.
- **The classifier is free text.** `ContribArchives`'s `Data releases` column spells this input 21
  ways across the decade; the matcher is a broad regex on "new home(s)"/"new-home", case-normalised
  and comma-tokenised but not otherwise cleaned. A vintage whose text silently omits the release
  would be misfiled into the baseline, biasing every test **toward** finding no difference.
- **All the nowcast work measures a model, not a market.** Every Δ describes the Atlanta Fed's
  estimate of GDP. `ContribArchives` ends 2026-07-28 and carries no Q4-2026 vintages, so `-1`'s class
  priors are out-of-sample for the quarter being nowcast.
- **The revision series is 33 pairs, archive-sourced, and not a clean difference.** The restated
  figure is the value as of the *next* release, which incorporates one month of survey revision
  **and** any seasonal or benchmark revision landing in between. Coverage is 2022–2026 with gaps,
  from the 124 of 130 Wayback captures that parsed — a set determined by what the archive holds, not
  by sampling. Both facts widen the true revision distribution rather than narrowing it. No ALFRED
  vintage series was attempted, on the sibling's finding that the endpoint 404s from these runners.
- **The component read is on revised data.** The inventory, months-supply and price figures come
  from FRED's current series, which is the *revised* basis — not the basis a day-one headline is on.
- **`symbols: []` is doing real work.** Even were every measurement twice as strong, this event has
  no instrument attached and no macro-keyed house playbook. ITB, XHB, DHI, LEN, PHM and TOL were
  measured because they are the obvious candidates, not because anything holds them.
