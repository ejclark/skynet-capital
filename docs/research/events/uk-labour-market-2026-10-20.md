## At a glance

**TL;DR.** **The wires will report a wage re-acceleration, and it will be the Bank of England's own
forecast printing on time.** Private-sector regular pay has fallen to **2.8%** y/y (Apr–Jun 2026) from
**2.9%** (Mar–May), and the market narrative is "UK wages are decelerating." But the **July 2026
Monetary Policy Report's own projection** is that private-sector AWE growth averages **2.8% in
2026 Q2 and around 3.0% in Q3** — it *turns up*. This release carries **Jun–Aug 2026**, two of whose
three months are Q3. So a **3.0%** print is the Bank's central case arriving exactly as forecast, and
reading it as a hawkish surprise is the error this document exists to prevent. That resets the
threshold: the sibling ledger for the **09-15** print put its wage kill at **≥3.3%**; on the Bank's own

2. **The Bank forecasts private-sector wage growth to RISE into the window this print covers — and
   that is the finding.** SUPPORTED on the direction, **search-relayed** on the figures, which is why
   this leg is graded medium rather than high. The **July 2026 Monetary Policy Report** projects annual
   private-sector AWE growth to average **2.8% in 2026 Q2** and **around 3.0% in Q3**, *"consistent with
   slack in the labour market continuing to weigh on wage growth"*, with private-sector regular AWE
   growth having eased to **2.9% in the three months to May**. The arithmetic that makes it bind here:
   this release carries **Jun–Aug 2026**, so **two of its three months (July, August) are Q3**. A print
   at **3.0%** is therefore the Bank's own central case landing on schedule. **The consequence is a
   threshold, not a story.** The [`uk-labour-market-2026-09-15`](uk-labour-market-2026-09-15.md) lane
   registered **≥3.3%** as the level at which the deceleration premise breaks — correctly, for a
   **May–Jul** window straddling the 2.8% Q2 average. Transplanting that number onto a **Jun–Aug**
   window whose forecast baseline is **0.2pp higher** would manufacture a false kill. This ledger sets
   **≥3.4%** and says why. Registered as **FT-uk-labour-market-2026-10-20-1**.

3. **The premise both proposals were filed on is calendar-true and forecast-false.** MIXED, and the
   mixed grade is the point. Both proposals call this *the last labour read before the Autumn Budget*.
   The Budget is **2026-10-28** (`uk-autumn-budget-2026-10-28`, HM Treasury primary, 31 July 2026) and
   the OBR publishes its **Economic and fiscal outlook** the same day — so on the calendar the claim is
   simply true. On the **forecast**, it is not. The OBR's own published timetables put the *initial
   economy forecast* with the Treasury **2025-09-17** for a **2025-11-26** Budget (**10 weeks**) and
   **2026-01-14** for a **2026-03-03** statement (**7 weeks**); the scrutiny process from there refines
   an economy forecast that is already closed, and the public guidance is that only *measures* — not
   data — arrive *"just over a week before the Budget."* A labour-market release landing **D-8** is on
   the wrong side of that door. **Named limit, loudly:** `obr.uk` **403**'d this lane, so no OBR page was
   read directly and the 2026 autumn timetable specifically was not obtainable; these are two dated
   precedents relayed through search, not the rule for this Budget. That is exactly why it is registered
   as a forward test rather than asserted — **FT-uk-labour-market-2026-10-20-2**, scored off the EFO
   itself on 10-28.

4. **The other half of the premise is true, and by a measurable margin.** SUPPORTED, two dated
   precedents plus one exact structural match. The Bank's MPR data cut-offs sit **days**, not weeks,
   before publication: the **November 2025** Report states *"All data are as of 28 October 2025"* and
   published **2025-11-06** (**9 days**); the **February 2026** Report averaged wholesale energy prices
