## At a glance

**TL;DR.** **Stand aside — and stop calling this "the shutdown edition." It is the horizon-roll
edition, and that is the bigger fact.** The proposal that created this event gave it to me as the
first STEO after the **2026-12-11** funding cliff, and flagged a second claim — that the January
edition rolls the forecast horizon forward a year — as *unverified*. Both are now settled from
primaries, and they settle in opposite directions. **The horizon roll is real:** EIA's own archived
editions show the indicator table's columns moving **2024|2025|2026** (November 2025) to
**2025|2026|2027** (January 2026), so **2027-01-12 publishes the first 2028 forecast lines** —
including the first EIA **2028 national electricity-consumption** number, the out-year
[`ai-energy-constraint.md`](../ai-energy-constraint.md)'s demand leg has never had. **The
publication risk is real but its headline analogue is void.** Exposure is confirmed — FY2027
Energy-Water never reached the House floor, so EIA sits inside the CR and a lapse from 2026-12-12
makes 01-12 **day 32**. But `cr-expiry-2026-12-11`'s **35-day 2018–19** analogue cannot be carried
here: Energy-Water was one of the five FY2019 bills enacted in the **September 2018** minibus, so
that shutdown **never touched EIA**. The analogue that applies is the **43-day 2025 lapse**, and it
is duration-matched and favourable: **both** editions inside it published — **October 7, 2025**
(day 7) and **November 12, 2025** (day 43). Finally, the corridor is the inverse of the December

the January FOMC blackout opens — which makes 01-12 the **cleanest attribution environment for a
STEO this calendar has**, and so the first honest out-of-sample test of the STEO-day null. The
event is **estimate**-dated with `symbols: []`; no date-keyed action is licensed by any of it.

right one is reassuring — and while checking that, the claim the proposing lane could *not* verify

rolls, so it prints the first 2028 lines this calendar will ever see.

### Conviction legs, tested

1. **The date is primary-sourced and doubly derived — SUPPORTED.** The raw table lists
   **01/12/2027** for the January edition, Notes cell **blank**, release window **12:00–12:15 pm
   ET**. The page's rule — "the first Tuesday following the first Thursday of each month", with the
   footnote "If a Federal holiday falls on the Monday before the normal release date the release is
   delayed until Wednesday" — reproduces it: **2027-01-07 is a Thursday**, the following Tuesday is
   **01-12**, and **2027-01-11 is a Monday that is not a Federal holiday** (New Year's Day 2027 is
   Friday **01-01**; MLK Day is Monday **01-18**). Filed **estimate** on prefix grounds only.

2. **The horizon roll is real — SUPPORTED, and this is the leg the proposing lane explicitly could
   not carry.** It wrote the claim down as "NOT verified from a primary by the proposing session and
   flagged for the owning lane to check rather than asserted." Checked, from EIA's own archived
   editions rather than any description of them. The *U.S. energy market indicators* table on page 2
   of each report carries three year columns:

   | Edition | Indicator-table columns | Source |
   |---|---|---|
   | November 2025 | **2024 \| 2025 \| 2026** | `archives/nov25.pdf`, text layer, cover-dated November 12, 2025 |
   | January 2026 | **2025 \| 2026 \| 2027** | `archives/jan26.pdf`, text layer, cover-dated January 13, 2026 |

   The January edition **drops the oldest year and adds an out-year**. The prose confirms it rather
   than the table alone: January 2026 forecasts Brent "$56/b in 2026 and **$54/b in 2027**", Henry
   Hub "$4.60/MMBtu in **2027**", and electricity consumption growth "1% in 2026 and **3% in 2027**"
   — 2027 figures that did not exist a report earlier. So **2027-01-12 is the first STEO to publish
   2028 lines**, and the current August 2026 edition's own two-column *Notable Forecast Changes*
   table (**2026 | 2027**) is consistent with a horizon that still ends in 2027 today.

3. **EIA really is exposed to a 2026-12-12 lapse — SUPPORTED, and this is the half of the proposal
   that survives.** The escape hatch would be full-year appropriations. There are none: CRFB's
   FY2027 status page (page-dated **2026-09-03**) records Energy-Water as "Approved by full committee
   markup on **5/20** by a vote of **34 to 25**" with **no floor action**, while the only FY2027
   bills through the full House are **MilCon-VA (5/15)**, **Agriculture (6/4)** and **National
   Security-State (7/15)**, and every Senate committee row reads **TBD**. The same page records the
   CR "**Signed by the President on 9/2**" — independently corroborating the signature that
   [`cr-expiry-2026-12-11`](cr-expiry-2026-12-11.md) and
   [`government-funding-deadline-2026-12-11`](government-funding-deadline-2026-12-11.md) both still
   carry as *unverified* (secondary corroboration; congress.gov 403s, recorded in `probe-ref.blocked`).
   So DOE/EIA is inside PL 119-103, and a lapse beginning **00:00 2026-12-12** makes **2027-01-12
   day 32**.

4. **The 2018–19 analogue does not transfer to publication risk — REFUTED, and it is the load-bearing
   correction here.** [`cr-expiry-2026-12-11`](cr-expiry-2026-12-11.md) reasons from the **35-day
   2018–19 lame-duck shutdown**, and the creating proposal carried that straight across, noting a
   lapse "would be ~32 days old by 01-12." The arithmetic is right and the inference is not:
   **Energy-Water was one of five full-year FY2019 bills enacted in the September 2018 minibus**, so
   the 2018–19 shutdown was *partial* and **DOE was funded throughout it**. The January 2019 STEO
   published into a fully-funded agency. The 2018–19 precedent is therefore evidence about
   **political duration** — which is all `cr-expiry` claims from it — and **no evidence whatever
   about whether EIA keeps publishing**. This ledger uses it for the first and never the second.

5. **The 2025 lapse is the right analogue, is duration-matched, and says EIA publishes — SUPPORTED.**
   The FY2026 lapse ran **2025-10-01 → 2025-11-12**, 43 days. Two STEO editions were scheduled inside
   it, and **both published on their scheduled dates**, verified from the reports themselves:

   | Edition | Scheduled | Archive PDF cover date | Day of lapse |

   | October 2025 | 10/07/2025 | **October 7, 2025** (`archives/oct25.pdf`) | **7** |
   | November 2025 | 11/12/2025 | **November 12, 2025** (`archives/nov25.pdf`) | **43** (its final day) |

   A lapse beginning 2026-12-12 puts this edition at **day 32** — inside a window EIA has
   demonstrably sustained. **The caution that survives is EIA's own wording, and it is not from
   2025.** `eia.gov/about/announcement.php`, fetched live today, is EIA's standing shutdown notice
   and it is **dated January 22, 2018**: *"EIA will be able to operate for a **short period of
   time** during the lapse in appropriations. Until further notice, the EIA.gov website will
   continue to be updated, and publications will continue to be released according to established
   schedules."* A web search attributed that text to the 2025 lapse; the primary shows it is an
   eight-year-old page describing the three-day January 2018 lapse. The sentence is EIA's own hedge
   about carryover balances, it has no stated duration, and it is the reason this leg's confidence
