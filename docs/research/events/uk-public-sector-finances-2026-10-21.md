### Conviction legs, tested

**1. The date and the 07:00 collision are both publisher primaries, fetched separately.**
**SUPPORTED**, and the second half is what the proposal cared about.
`ons.gov.uk/releases/publicsectorfinancesukseptember2026` reads *"Public sector finances, UK:
September 2026"*, *"21 October 2026 at 7:00am"*, contents *"net borrowing net cash requirement and net
debt"*, marked *"This release is not yet published."*
`ons.gov.uk/releases/consumerpriceinflationukseptember2026` reads *"Consumer price inflation, UK:
September 2026"*, *"21 October 2026 7:00am"*, also not yet published. Two independent publisher pages,
one minute — the co-print is not an inference. Publication-cadence arithmetic corroborates: **70**
consecutive PSF bulletins from 2020-11-20 to 2026-08-21 all fall on the **19th–26th** of the month,
and 2026-10-21 is inside that band. The `estimate` label is the taxonomy gap and the no-self-confirm
limit, not a doubt.

**2. The proposal's substance premise is inverted — the OBR's fiscal forecast closes about a month
before Budget day.** **MIXED**, graded down for relay, and it is this document's central finding.
The OBR's own **November 2025 forecast timetable** records that the window for market determinants in
the final pre-measures **economy** forecast was the ten working days to **2025-10-10**, and the window
for interest rates in the final pre-measures **fiscal** forecast was the ten working days to
**2025-10-21** — **47** and **36 days** respectively before the **2025-11-26** Budget — with *"the
remaining stages of the forecast, that incorporate the direct effect of the Chancellor's policy
announcements"* completed **2025-11-21**, i.e. **measures, not data**. Independently, the **October
2024** EFO's pre-measures forecast was *"finalised on 26 September"* for a **2024-10-30** Budget —
**34 days**. The arithmetic that makes this bind: **34–36 days before 2026-10-28 is 2026-09-22 to
2026-09-24**. The **2026-09-22** print (August data) sits on that boundary; **this** print, at
**D-7**, sits roughly four weeks past it. The 2024 round is the exact structural analogue and it
settles the direction: September borrowing published **2024-10-22**, Budget **2024-10-30**, forecast
closed **2024-09-26** — a **D-8** print that could not have been in it. **Named limit, loudly:**
`obr.uk` **403**'d this lane twice, so no OBR page was read directly and the **autumn 2026** timetable
specifically was never obtained; both precedents are **search relays** of the OBR's own published
material. That is exactly why this is registered as **FT-…-1** rather than asserted.

**3. The correction is not a downgrade — it is a better description of what the print does.**
**SUPPORTED**, from the ONS's own bulletin text. Every PSF bulletin compares the month's outturn to a
named OBR profile; the **July 2026** bulletin states its comparisons use *"the Economic and fiscal
outlook — March 2026 report"* (published 3 March 2026). So on **2026-10-21** the bulletin will report
financial-year-to-September borrowing against a **March 2026** profile that is **replaced seven days
later**. Two consequences worth writing down. First, this print is the **last out-of-sample
observation the public gets before the EFO** — the first number capable of showing that the forecast
the Chancellor is about to be scored against was already drifting. Second, its own *"versus forecast"*
headline is the least informative line in it, because the forecast is about to change: an overshoot on
10-21 is not a Budget-day surprise, it is an input the OBR has already reprofiled around.

**5. The co-print is genuinely rare, and the attribution worry survives while the pricing worry does
not.** **SUPPORTED**, and this is the honest amendment to the proposal's mechanics claim. Over the
**30 months** where both ONS listings are complete (2024-03-20 → 2026-08-21, 30 CPI prints and 30 PSF

prints fell exactly **one calendar day** from a CPI print, so near-misses are the norm and collisions
are not: 2026-10-21 is the **second** exact co-print in 32 months. But the same window says the
collision does not create a pricing hazard, because neither release is a mover on its own: on IGLT.L,
**CPI-only** days run **1.25x** an ordinary day (t = **1.44**, n = 30) and **PSF-only** days **1.24x**
(t = **0.74**, n = 29) — statistically indistinguishable from each other and from noise. **The single
historical co-print is confounded and is reported as such:** on 2024-05-22 IGLT.L closed **-0.591%**
and the FTSE **-0.549%**, but that was also the day April CPI printed **2.3%** *and* the Prime
Minister called a general election. One observation, three candidate causes — it corroborates the
attribution rule and proves nothing about size.

### Honest limits

**First, the central finding is search-relayed, and that is why it is a forward test.** `obr.uk`
returned **403** to this lane twice (recorded in `probe-ref.blocked`), so neither the November 2025
forecast timetable nor the October 2024 EFO nor the autumn 2026 timetable was read directly. The
34-day and 36-day figures in leg 2 are relays of the OBR's own published material through search, and
the autumn-2026 timetable — the only one that actually governs 2026-10-28 — was never obtained at all.
Leg 2 is graded **MIXED** and registered as **FT-…-1** for exactly this reason.

**Second, the fiscal-forecast close date and the outturn cut-off are not the same object, and this
document does not pretend they are.** What the November 2025 timetable records is the window for
*interest rates and market determinants* in the pre-measures fiscal forecast. An outturn data cut-off
could in principle sit later. The 2024 round is the stronger evidence precisely because it names the
whole pre-measures forecast as *"finalised on 26 September"*; but two rounds is two rounds, and a
convention inferred from them is a hypothesis about how the OBR works, not a rule.

**Third, the composition split in leg 6 is a parliamentary secondary, not a primary.** The
receipts-**+£2.8bn** / spending-**+£5.1bn** decomposition came through a relay of the House of Lords
Library's fiscal-outlook briefing, whose page **403**'d this lane. The headline figures around it —
£56.7bn, +£2.3bn, 94.1%, the £3.9bn CGNCR overshoot — are all **fetched from the ONS bulletin
directly** and are not affected.

**Fourth, the market study's limits.** IGLT.L is an **all-maturity** gilt index, so it under-weights
the long end where UK fiscal stress actually shows; it stops trading at 16:30 London, so it cannot see
the 07:00 print in isolation at all — every number in leg 4 is a **daily close-to-close** move, and a
07:00 release's signature is diluted by a full session of everything else. `GBPUSD=X` is a Yahoo spot
series with **56** usable bars against IGLT.L's 70, and its daily-close timing is not dependable; its
row is reported for completeness and no weight is placed on it. **n = 70** is a decent sample for a
null but rules out only the effect sizes that would matter here, not a small one. And leg 5's
significant-looking CPI-only result on GBP/USD (0.60x, t = -3.68) is **not carried** anywhere in this
document, for the same data-quality reason.

**Fifth, this release has no statutory-register corroboration in this document.** The labour-market
entries carry a gov.uk pre-announcement page reading *"(confirmed)"*; the expected slug for this one
**404**'d, so the date rests on the ONS release-calendar page plus cadence arithmetic. That is a
publisher primary and it is enough for `estimate`, but it is one source where the siblings have two.

**Sixth, no consensus or whisper was findable** for the September borrowing figure, so leg 6's
expectation is a read against the OBR's March profile and the four months of drift already published —
not against a survey. And leg 10 is a **null claim**: its failure mode is a channel that exists and is
simply not instrumented in this book.
