## At a glance

**TL;DR.** **This print is one subtraction, and the subtraction is already tilted up.** August 2025
CPI rose **0.3% m/m** (ONS, fetched), so the August 2026 annual rate is simply July's **2.9%** plus
whatever this August clears **+0.3%** by. Three primaries say it clears it: air fares rose only
**11.7%** m/m in July 2026 against **30.2%** a year earlier, so the August unwind — the *largest
downward contributor* to the Aug-2025 annual rate — has far less to hand back; the **Ofgem cap does
not step** between 1 July and 1 October, so gas and electricity are flat m/m **by construction**;
and petrol reached about **162p/l** in August. Food at **1.3%** and falling is the only real offset.
Base case **3.0–3.2%**, squarely inside the Bank's own July-MPR path (peak **~3.2% in 2026 Q4**).
**The trap is that air fares sit inside services** — so the same mechanical base effect that lifts
the headline also lifts **services**, which is the one number Greene, Mann and Pill read before they
vote 24 hours later. A hot services print here is **arithmetic, not second-round effects**, and this
entry's job is to say so before someone trades the confusion. **None of this is ours to trade** —

## Initial research

### The question, plainly

This event was added on **placement**, not on content — discovered 2026-09-09 in the
[`boe-decision-2026-09-17`](boe-decision-2026-09-17.md) initial research, which noted only that the
MPC's last two inputs are the 09-15 labour market overview and this 09-16 CPI print. So the question
is not "is UK inflation interesting" but: **is there anything in this specific print that is not
already implied by arithmetic the market can do, and does any of it reach a US-only,
equity-and-earnings-keyed book?**

**One-line verdict:** the headline is close to mechanically determined and therefore near-dead
information — but the **services** line is not, and the reason services could run hot is an
**air-fare base effect that will be misread as second-round inflation** by a committee with three
standing dissenters voting the next morning. That misreading risk, plus a clean **attribution**
job on a very crowded 09-16, is what this entry is for.

### Conviction legs, tested

1. **The headline reduces to one subtraction, and both terms are primary.** SUPPORTED. The ONS
   **July 2026** bulletin (fetched today): *"The Consumer Prices Index (CPI) rose by 2.9% in the 12
   months to July 2026, up from 2.6% the previous month"*, with *"CPI rose by 0.3% in July 2026"*
   m/m. The ONS **August 2025** bulletin (fetched today): *"On a monthly basis, CPI rose by 0.3% in
   August 2025, the same rate as in August 2024."* So the August 2026 annual rate is
   **2.9% + (Aug-26 m/m − 0.3pp)**, and every remaining leg is an argument about one monthly number.
   This is worth stating because most previews will argue about the annual rate directly, which is
   the harder and less informative question.

2. **The air-fare base effect is the single biggest known push, and it pushes UP.** SUPPORTED,
   primary on both sides of the comparison. The ONS **July 2026** bulletin: *"Air fares rose by
   11.7% between June and July 2026, compared with a rise of 30.2% between the same two months in
   2025."* The ONS **August 2025** bulletin: *"Air fares made the largest downward contribution to
   the monthly change in both CPIH and CPI annual rates."* Read together: last year a **30.2%**
   July spike unwound hard in August and dragged the annual rate down; this year the July spike was
   **roughly a third of that size**, so there is far less to unwind. A shallower August fall than
   last year's is arithmetically an **upward** contribution to the annual rate. **What is not
   knowable in advance:** the exact August 2026 air-fare move — the ONS does not publish the
   Aug-2025 m/m magnitude in the passage fetched, so this leg is established on *direction and
   relative size*, not on a number.

3. **Energy is flat month-on-month by construction — the July step is already in.** SUPPORTED,
   primary. Ofgem's cap steps on 1 January, 1 April, 1 July and 1 October; the **1 July 2026** step
   was a **13%** rise (press-cited) and it is what produced July's jump — the ONS July bulletin puts
   *"The 12-month rate for housing and household services … 4.1% in July 2026, up from 2.7% in
   June"*, with gas *"up 14.7% … compared with a fall of 7.2% a year ago"* and electricity *"up
   3.6% … compared with a fall of 3.8% a year ago"*. **The next step is 1 October** (Ofgem press
   release, 26 August 2026, fetched today: *"a 4% increase of the energy price cap for the period
   covering 1 October to 31 December 2026"*, to **£1,723** from **£1,663**). So between the July and
   August collections there is **no cap change at all**: gas and electricity contribute ~nothing to
   the August m/m, while their elevated **y/y** carries over unchanged. That is a rare piece of
   certainty in a monthly print and it removes the largest source of variance.

4. **Motor fuel pushes up; the fuel-duty rise does not, and the distinction is a date.** MIXED —
   direction supported, sourcing weaker. Press reporting (fetched today, aggregator-grade) puts UK
   petrol at about **162p/l** in August 2026, described as the highest of the year, following the
   crude surge this calendar already tracks (Brent **$99.41** on 09-08 per sibling ledgers). Against
   an Aug-2025 base in which *"restaurants and hotels, and motor fuels made large, partially
   offsetting, upward contributions"*, the fuel push is real but **not** as clean as leg 2's. **The
   correction this leg exists to make:** the **1p fuel-duty rise took effect 2026-09-01** — it is in
   the **September** data, published 21 October, **not** in this print. A preview that folds it in
   is double-counting.

5. **Food is the honest offset, and it is small.** SUPPORTED, primary. ONS July 2026: *"Food and
   non-alcoholic beverage prices rose by 1.3% in the 12 months to July 2026, down from 1.7% the
   previous month."* A decelerating food line is a genuine drag on the annual rate, but at a **1.3%**
   run-rate on a ~11% weight it is arithmetically dominated by legs 2 and 4. It is why the base case
   is **3.0–3.2%** rather than 3.3%+.

6. **The result sits inside the Bank's own forecast, which is why it should not move the decision.**
   SUPPORTED. The **July 2026** Monetary Policy Report (fetched today) states CPI *"is likely to rise
   over the rest of this year as higher global energy prices continue to feed through"*, with the
   central projection peaking at **~3.2% in 2026 Q4** (press-relayed from the 30 July round) and the
   Treasury forecast panel (surveyed 19 August) at **3.3%** for Q4. A 3.0–3.2% August print is
   therefore **on the Bank's path, not a surprise to it**, which is precisely why it cannot rescue
   the 6.9% hike tail: centralbank.watch (re-fetched today, data 09-08) has the 09-17 meeting at
   **hold 93.1% · hike 6.9% · cut 0.0%**, implied **3.77%** against Bank Rate **3.75%**.

7. **Services is the live number, and the trap is that air fares are inside it.** SUPPORTED on the
   mechanism, genuinely two-sided on the outcome — **this is the ledger's finding**. Services CPI
   was **3.4%** in July, down from **3.6%** in June and **4.4%** in January (ONS). The July majority's
   entire case rests on that deceleration: *"There is little evidence so far to suggest such
   effects"* on second rounds, with disinflation *"supported by a loose labour market and slowing
   wage growth"*. But air transport is a **services** component, so leg 2's mechanical push lands
   on the exact line the three dissenters are reading. A services print of **3.6–3.8%** driven
   entirely by a shallow air-fare unwind would look, on the tape and in a headline, indistinguishable
   from the second-round pressure Greene, Mann and Pill argued for at 6–3 in July. **The honest
   other side:** it could also simply *be* broadening — Citi/YouGov's August survey has 12-month
   public inflation expectations jumping **3.4% → 3.9%** and long-term to **4.1%** (press-cited),
   which is the dissenters' strongest new evidence and runs against this leg's read.

8. **The government has already pre-announced a −0.10pp cut to CPI, and it lands after the November
   vote.** SUPPORTED, **primary and quantified by the issuer**. gov.uk, published **21 July 2026**
   (fetched today): the PM cut VAT on domestic electricity from **5% to 0%** effective **1 October**,
   *"around £45 off the yearly Ofgem price cap in October"*, and — the line that matters —
   *"estimated to reduce CPI inflation by around 0.10 percentage points and RPI by around 0.14
   percentage points"*. Independently reflected in Ofgem's own 26 August cap release: *"The
   Government's decision to remove VAT from all domestic electricity bills is reflected in this
   update"* and *"the VAT reduction means electricity bills will remain broadly stable"* while gas
   rises **8%**. **The timing is the whole point:** October prices are published **mid-November**,
   *after* the 05 November MPC meeting — so the committee forecasts it rather than observes it, and
   the strip that prices **94.6%** odds of a higher Bank Rate by **2026-12-17** (implied **4.11%**)
   is pricing through a known, dated, officially-sized disinflationary step. **Two caveats kept on
   the record:** it is stated as running only to **31 March 2027** with extension to be decided at
   the 28 October Budget (so the same 0.10pp reverses in April unless renewed), and Northern Ireland
   is excluded, so the effect is GB-weighted.

9. **The print's practical value to this calendar is attribution on a crowded 09-16.** SUPPORTED,
   mechanical. Thirty-six tracked events sit within five days of this date. On **09-16 alone**: this
   print at **02:00 ET**, the **Bund 30Y auction**, **US retail sales** and **import/export prices**
   at 08:30 ET, **NAHB HMI** at 10:00, the **TIC monthly**, **VIX expiration**, the **Google adtech
   opinion unseal**, and the **FOMC statement at 14:00 ET**. A pre-FOMC move in the global long end
   on 09-16 therefore has a named alternative cause that is neither the Fed nor US data — the same
   discipline the [`boe-decision-2026-09-17`](boe-decision-2026-09-17.md) ledger imposes on 09-17,
   pulled one day earlier.

10. **No tracked symbol carries a sterling or UK-inflation channel — and no play is proposed.**
    SUPPORTED, and this is where the honesty sits rather than a hedge. `symbols: []`. The house
    playbooks (S1/S2/E1/S3/S4 + G1, [`trade-playbooks.md`](../../plans/trade-playbooks.md)) are
    equity- and earnings-keyed; none is rates-keyed, let alone keyed to a foreign CPI release. The
    one channel that exists is **global term premium**, which
    [`fomc-2026-09-16`](fomc-2026-09-16.md) already tiers for NVDA/AVGO/MRVL/CRWV, and it is
    second-order at best from a UK print. The **impact tier was reconsidered and left at `low`** —
    the argument for `medium` is real (it is the input to a `medium` event and it does an attribution
    job), but a UK-only print with no instrument in this book does not earn the same cadence as the
    decision it feeds.
