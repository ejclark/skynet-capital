## Initial research

### The question, plainly

Three questions. (1) The [`dallas-fed-mfg-2026-11-30`](dallas-fed-mfg-2026-11-30.md) sibling
*discovered* this event four days ago and filed it on one line of reasoning — that services is
"where this cycle's inflation stickiness lives." Is that true, and is it true of *this* survey?
(2) The print lands 10:30 ET on **2026-12-01**, one business day behind its own manufacturing twin
and 30 minutes behind two bigger releases: how much information is actually left in it by then?
(3) Does anything justify moving off the twin's permanent stand-aside?

**One-line verdict:** the survey is far more redundant than the calendar entry assumed — same field
window as the twin, released one business day later, headline correlation **+0.893** over 235
months — but that redundancy is a *full-sample* artifact that **collapses to r² 0.466 on 2023–2026**,
and the live divergence is a services wage index that **rose** to 18.8 (above the primary's own
stated 15.4 average) in the same month manufacturing wages fell 10 points, with employment at the
19th percentile underneath it; the stand-aside is unchanged and on 12-01 becomes structural, because
ISM Manufacturing and JOLTS both print 30 minutes earlier.

1. **The date is quotable verbatim from the primary — SUPPORTED, and it stays `estimate`.**
   dallasfed.org's TSSOS page carries a table headed "2026 release dates" with the column header

   strive to ensure the information is published to our website in a timely manner, we cannot
   guarantee the exact timing all survey content becomes available online."* Its rows read
   `January | Tuesday, Jan. 27` … `October | Tuesday, Oct. 27` · **`November | Tuesday, Dec. 1`** ·
   `December | Tuesday, Dec. 29`. The **July report independently prints "Next release: Sept. 1,
   2026"** and the August report **"Next release: Sept. 29, 2026"** — two in-document confirmations
   of the table's own spill pattern. 9:30 a.m. CT = **10:30 ET**. The entry stays `estimate` on the
   binding ground the twin identified and this doc re-confirms: **`market-events-data.ts`'s
   source-prefix table has no confirmed tier for a reserve-bank survey page at all** (`FED:` is
   scoped to federalreserve.gov's FOMC calendar), so no source quality can promote this series from
   any lane. That is a governance observation, not a research finding, and is left as one.

2. **What the survey is, and why `low` is right — SUPPORTED, with one correction to the twin's
   framing.** A monthly diffusion survey of Texas *service-sector* firms, seasonally adjusted,
   reported as (% increase − % decrease). August's edition: **236 of 340** firms responded — a
   respondent panel **more than three times** the manufacturing survey's (69 of 112), which is worth
   recording because the twin's `low` filing rested partly on a thin panel and that reason does not
   transfer. `low` is still right here, but for a different reason: **position in the release
   stack**, not sample size (leg 10).

3. **A composition break landed this year and it bounds every long-run statistic below —
   SUPPORTED.** The August report states verbatim: *"Starting with the January 2026 data release,
   the Texas Retail Outlook Survey results will no longer be published. The retail sector will
   continue to be included in the Texas Service Sector Outlook Survey results."* So from Jan-2026
   the published series is a merged services+retail read where the earlier history was not, and
   the same release carried the **annual seasonal-factor revision** (*"Annual seasonal revisions
   result in slight changes in the seasonally adjusted series"*). Every number in this doc is
   computed on the **post-revision vintage downloaded 2026-09-05**; a pre-August-2026 quote of the
   same statistic may differ, and the close-out must re-download rather than trust these.

4. **The redundancy is real, and larger than the calendar entry assumed — SUPPORTED, from an
   original pairing.** Two mechanisms, both sourced. **First, one field window, not two.** The
   Aug-2026 TSSOS report says *"Data were collected August 18–26"* — the identical string the
   manufacturing report carries for the same month. Checked across four more: Nov-25 *"Nov. 10–18"*,
   Jan-26 *"Jan. 13–21"*, Jun-26 *"June 16–24"*, Jul-26 *"July 14–22"* — all identical to the
   windows the twin's ledger sourced. **The 12-01 print therefore contains no observation collected
   after the 11-30 print's.** Second, the headlines co-move:

   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | general business activity | general business activity | **+0.893** | 0.797 | 235 |
   | wages & benefits | wages & benefits | +0.852 | 0.725 | 235 |
   | company outlook | company outlook | +0.837 | 0.700 | 235 |
   | finished-goods / selling prices | selling prices | +0.793 | 0.629 | 235 |
   | raw-materials / input prices | input prices | +0.749 | 0.561 | 235 |
   | employment | employment | +0.722 | 0.522 | 235 |
   | production | revenue | +0.663 | 0.440 | 235 |

5. **…but the redundancy is a full-sample artifact and does not hold in the current regime —
   SUPPORTED, and this is the doc's main contribution.** Splitting the same pairing by era:

   | Window | n | level r | r² | change r | same-direction |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | Full 2007–2026 | 235 | +0.893 | 0.797 | +0.711 | 64.5% |
   | ex-2008/2009/2020 | 199 | +0.842 | 0.710 | +0.551 | 63.6% |
   | 2021–2026 | 68 | +0.893 | 0.798 | +0.585 | 70.1% |
   | **2023–2026** | **44** | **+0.683** | **0.466** | **+0.360** | 65.1% |

   Crisis months (GFC, COVID) hit both sectors at once and inflate the level correlation; strip them
   and r² falls from 0.797 to 0.710. Restrict to the **last 44 months** and the manufacturing print
   explains **under half** the services level and **barely a third** of the month-to-month change.
   Note that even at full sample the *change* correlation is much weaker than the level one
   (0.711 vs 0.893) and the two indexes move the same direction only **64.5%** of months — a third
   of the time, the services print genuinely disagrees with the one released the day before.

6. **August 2026 is one of those disagreements, and a rare kind — SUPPORTED.** Manufacturing
   general business activity jumped **+10.3 to 11.6**; services *fell* **−2.4 to 4.2**. A month with
   manufacturing up ≥ +8 while services falls has occurred **6 times in 234 months (2.6%)**, and the
   full list is short enough to print: **Nov-15, Dec-17, Aug-19, Aug-24, Jan-25, Aug-26.** After the
   first five, the services index *rose* the following month **4 of 5** times, mean **+2.80** — that
   is **n=5, an anecdote**, recorded because it is the only conditioning evidence that exists and
   because stating it now stops a later session retrofitting it.

7. **Wages is the live line, and the twin's conclusion inverts here — SUPPORTED, and it is the
   reason to read this report.** The manufacturing sibling moved its reading order *off* wages
   because that survey's wage index fell 10 points to 21.1 (its own series median). The services
   survey's August text says the opposite, verbatim: *"The wages and benefits edged up three points
   to 18.8, a level that exceeds the series average of 15.4."* Percentiles of the 236-month history:
   wages **18.8 → 72nd**, input prices **35.6 → 81st**, hours worked **5.7 → 77th**, selling prices
   **8.9 → 61st**, headline activity **4.2 → 52nd**, revenue **6.6 → 30th**, employment **0.8 →
   19th**, capex **6.0 → 21st**. And the *path* is the finding, not the level: services wages ran
   **6.2 → 15.3 → 15.7 → 18.8** across May–August, a three-month rise of **+12.6** that ranks **4th
   of 233** monthly observations (1.7%) — the other three being **Jun-20, Jul-20 and May-21**, i.e.
   every prior instance in the series is a COVID-reopening artifact. **Persistence, honestly split:**
   conditioning on the *level* (≥18.8, n=65) the index is still ≥15.4 at t+3 in **60/65 = 92%** and
   still ≥18.8 in **43/65 = 66%**; conditioning on the *spike* (a ≥+12.6 three-month rise, n=3) it
   holds ≥15.4 in only **1/3**. Those disagree, and the disagreement is why this quarter's call is
   graded Medium rather than High: the n=65 level base rate is the usable one, the n=3 spike base
   rate is entirely COVID and carries no weight, and pretending the first exists without the second
   would be the dishonest version.

8. **Employment is the soft leg, and it is where November can actually surprise — SUPPORTED.** The
   services employment index printed **0.8** in August, down two points, the **19th percentile** of
   236 months against a series mean of 5.66. The index has been outright negative in only **40 of
   236 months (17%)** — but from a reading **≤1.0** (n=47) it is negative three months later in
   **26 of 47 = 55%**, mean t+3 **−2.36**. Aug→Nov is exactly t+3. The trailing twelve months are
   choppy around zero (−4.1, −6.1, 2.8, −1.1, 0.9, 0.4, 2.5, −1.0, −3.2, 8.1, 2.6, 0.8), so this is
   a labour market that has been flat-to-shrinking for a year while wages accelerated — the
   combination that makes the wage line worth watching rather than dismissing as demand-driven.

9. **No November seasonal survives the seasonal adjustment — REFUTED, and stated because a reader
   would look for it.** Mean November m/m change across the SA series, against the series' own
   monthly standard deviation: headline activity **+0.33** (n=19, σ 10.43 → **+0.14 se**), revenue
   **+0.22** (σ 9.41 → **+0.10 se**), wages **−0.53** (σ 4.21 → **−0.55 se**). All noise. A large
   November effect would have been evidence the adjustment was broken, not an edge.

10. **The 12-01 configuration is rare, computable, and it is what makes this print unreadable —
    SUPPORTED.** The services survey publishes one business day after the manufacturing survey,
    which publishes on the **last Monday**. When that Monday is also the month's **last business
    day**, the services release spills onto the **1st of the next month** — which is by construction
    **ISM Manufacturing day**. That spill happens in **35 of the 240 months** from Jan-2007 through
    Dec-2026, **14.6%**, and 2026 contains exactly two: **August data → Sept 1** and **November data
    → Dec 1**. So the 12-01 stack is ISM Manufacturing **10:00 (`high`, estimate)**, JOLTS **10:00

    window, behind two larger releases, on a morning the Fed is in **blackout (11-28 → 12-10,
    confirmed)** so no official voice can reinterpret any of them. **No tape observation on 12-01
    can be attributed to this survey even in principle**, and that is the honest reason it is filed
    `low`, superseding the panel-size reasoning in leg 2.

11. **The rehearsal already happened, four days ago, and it measured nothing — SUPPORTED.**

12. **The methodological finding: read the session, not the day — SUPPORTED, and it changes the
    instrument.** On 09-01 the close-to-close ranks (62nd/55th) and the open-to-close ranks

### What the conditions support

Nothing directional. What the primary buys is **a corrected purpose for this print and a corrected
instrument for reading it**. The purpose: not a second look at Texas activity — the twin already
gave you that, from the same field window, a day earlier, with 80% of the headline shared — but the
**services wage line**, which moved the *opposite way* from manufacturing's in August and now sits
above the series average the publisher itself names, with an employment index at the 19th percentile
underneath it. That combination is what a services-inflation read is actually for, and it is the one
thing the manufacturing survey structurally cannot tell you. The instrument: measure the **session,
not the day**, and accept that on 12-01 even a perfect session measurement scores the ISM/JOLTS/TSSOS
stack rather than this survey — which is why the tests registered below are about the **survey's
content**, where falsification is possible, and only one is about the tape. None of that is a
position. This series' job in the calendar is structural: the services counterweight to Chicago and
Dallas manufacturing, so a goods-only regional surprise and a broad one are distinguishable.

### Honest limits

200 today) — a later session may be back on secondaries. The base rates in legs 5–8 are
**in-sample over one series' whole history** with no out-of-sample holdout, and the 2023–2026 cut
that carries the doc's main claim is **n=44** — small enough that the decoupling could be sampling
noise rather than regime change, which is exactly why it is written as a refusal to assume
redundancy rather than a claim of independence. Leg 3's composition break means the pre-2026 and

second. The November field window is **derived, not fetched** — no November report exists — and is
±1 day on the 2025-vs-2026 gap difference. Leg 10's configuration count is a **calendar computation**
assuming the last-Monday rule holds back to 2007; the rule is validated on 2026 only, and the

Leg 11's tape work is **n=8 with a z under 1**, on **daily** bars — Yahoo returned HTTP 429 to every
